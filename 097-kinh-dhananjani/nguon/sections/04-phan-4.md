## mn97:30.15
- **PLI**: “Cātumahārājikehi, bho sāriputta, devehi tāvatiṁsā devā seyyo”ti.
- **EN**: “The gods of the thirty-three.”
- **NOTE**: The “gods of the thirty-three” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>tāvatiṁsā devā</i>), who are subjects of Sakka (also called Indra), enjoy refined sensual delights. The number is a reduplication of the trinity. In Buddhist texts they are not enumerated, but Yājñavalkya reckons them as eight Vasus, eleven Rudras, twelve Ādityas, plus Indra and Prajāpati (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.9.2). The final pair are elsewhere said to be Dyaus (“Heaven” = Zeus) and Pṛthivī (“Earth”), or the twin Aśvins.

## mn97:30.18
- **PLI**: “Tāvatiṁsehi, bho sāriputta, devehi yāmā devā seyyo”ti.
- **EN**: “The gods of Yama.”
- **NOTE**: Gods in this realm (spelled <i lang='pi' translate='no'>yāma</i>, “of Yama”) are subjects of Yama, the god who guards the paths to the land of the dead (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn1.33/en/sujato#10.3'>SN 1.33:10.3</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn130/en/sujato#3.1'>MN 130:3.1</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dhp235/en/sujato'>Dhp 235</a>). He was the son of Vivasvant (the sun) and brother of Manu, and as the first person to die, Rig Veda 10.4 tells us that he discovered the paths to the lands of the dead that he now guards with his pair of hounds. Yama is a righteous god, but sinners see him as horrifying due to their own guilt (Brahma Purāṇa 106.46).

- **NOTE**: The “joyful gods” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>tusitā devā</i>) were known to the Jains, who classed them among the gods of the world’s end (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>lokāntika</i>; Tattvārthasūtra 4.25; Viyāhapaṇṇatti 5.3.95). Later Hindu tradition says they were the sons of Kaśyapa through Aditi, otherwise known as the twelve Ādityas: Viṣṇu, Śakra, Tvaṣṭṛ, Dhātṛ, Aryaman, Pūṣan, Vivasvat, Savitṛ, Mitra, Varuṇa, Bhaga, and Aṁśu. (Agnipurāṇa 19.1–3, Liṅgapurāṇa 63.22b–26). In Buddhism, this realm is famed as the final home of Bodhisattas before they descend to the human realm to become Buddhas.

## mn97:30.24
- **PLI**: “Tusitehi, bho sāriputta, devehi nimmānaratī devā seyyo”ti.
- **EN**: “The gods who love to create.”
- **NOTE**: These deities (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>nimmānaratī devā</i>) are said to create whatever delights they wish and enjoy them, living in a world of their own projection. They seem to be primarily Buddhist, perhaps influenced by such passages as Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.9, which says that in dreams, on the boundary between this world and the next, one creates (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>svayaṁ nirmāya</i>) a new reality that is revealed by one’s inner light. They are, however, mentioned in later texts such as Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.2.30 as one of three groups of thirty, and Mārkaṇḍeyapurāṇa 44, which adds that they preside over the months, seasons, and days.
