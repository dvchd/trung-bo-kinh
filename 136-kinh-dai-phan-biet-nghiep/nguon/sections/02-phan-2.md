## mn136:9.5

- **EN**: For I have seen a individual here who killed living creatures … and had wrong view. And when their body broke up, after death, they were reborn in a place of loss, a bad place, the underworld, hell.’
- **NOTE**: Having framed their hypothesis, the theorist presents their supporting observations.

## mn136:9.6
- **PLI**: So evamāha:
- **EN**: They say:

## mn136:9.7
- **PLI**: ‘yo kira, bho, pāṇātipātī adinnādāyī …pe… micchādiṭṭhi, sabbo so kāyassa bhedā paraṁ maraṇā apāyaṁ duggatiṁ vinipātaṁ nirayaṁ upapajjati.
- **EN**: ‘It seems that everyone who kills living creatures … and has wrong view is reborn in hell.
- **NOTE**: Now the theorist draws their inference, committing two logical fallacies. One is the fallacy of hasty generalization: the fact that <em>some people</em> who do bad deeds go to hell does not imply that <em>all people</em> who do bad deeds go to hell. The second is the post hoc fallacy: the fact that the bad deed <em>preceded</em> rebirth in hell does not imply that the bad deed <em>caused</em> rebirth in hell. The Buddha avoids the fallacy of hasty generalization by surveying a much larger, statistically significant scope, said to be ninety-one eons (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn71/en/sujato#14.2'>MN 71:14.2</a>). And he avoids the post hoc fallacy by flipping the causal arrow: if the <em>presence</em> of kamma leads to rebirth, then the <em>absence</em> of kamma should lead to the ending of rebirth. By practicing to realize the ending of kamma he confirmed this for himself.

## mn136:9.8
- **PLI**: Ye evaṁ jānanti, te sammā jānanti; ye aññathā jānanti, micchā tesaṁ ñāṇan’ti.
- **EN**: Those whose notion is this have the right notion. Those whose notion is otherwise have a wrong notion.’
- **NOTE**: Leaving behind caution and going beyond observation, the theorist now asserts that the results of their reasoning are the truth. | Here words from the root <i lang='pi' translate='no'>√ñā</i> are used for both right and wrong ideas. Normally I translate <i lang='pi' translate='no'>√ñā</i> as “knowledge”, but in English one cannot “know” something false. One might <em>believe</em> that it is nighttime when it is in fact the day, but one cannot <em>know</em> this. Usually <i lang='pi' translate='no'>√ñā</i> does have the positive sense of “true knowledge”, but when dealing with errant theorizers we also find it used for “false knowledge”, in which case I use “notion” (eg. <a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp4.13/en/sujato#17.2'>Snp 4.13:17.2</a>).

## mn136:9.9
- **PLI**: Iti so yadeva tassa sāmaṁ ñātaṁ sāmaṁ diṭṭhaṁ sāmaṁ viditaṁ tadeva tattha thāmasā parāmāsā abhinivissa voharati: ‘idameva saccaṁ, moghamaññan’ti.
- **EN**: And so they obstinately stick to what they have known, seen, and understood for themselves, insisting that: ‘This is the only truth, anything else is futile.’
- **NOTE**: Contemplatives sometimes deprecate logic as inferior to meditative insight. By not exercising the critical skills to evaluate and improve their reasoning, they become especially prone to using poor logic. The brilliance of meditative experiences can be overwhelming and it is easy to be convinced of the truth of one’s inferences, without even recognizing them as inferences. Sutta passages such as this show that the interpretation of meditative experiences must be tempered with clear logic.
