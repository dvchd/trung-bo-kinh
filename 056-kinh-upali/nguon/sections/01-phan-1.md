## mn56:1.2
- **PLI**: ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā nāḷandāyaṁ viharati pāvārikambavane.
- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying near Nāḷandā in Pāvārika’s Mango Grove.
- **NOTE**: Pāvārika means “blanket-weaver”. Places were often named for their primary economic activity. Probably there was a community of blanket weavers and the park was owned by their leading merchant.

## mn56:2.1  `nya2`
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena nigaṇṭho nāṭaputto nāḷandāyaṁ paṭivasati mahatiyā nigaṇṭhaparisāya saddhiṁ.
- **EN**: At that time the Jain ascetic of the Ñātika clan was residing at Nāḷandā together with a large assembly of Jain ascetics.
- **NOTE**: Jain tradition holds that Mahāvīra passed at a Pāvā near Nāḷandā. Buddhist texts know only the Pāvā of the Mallians to the north. Evidently there has been some confusion, and perhaps “Pāvārika’s mango grove”, the site of discussions with Jains here and at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn42.8/en/sujato'>SN 42.8</a> and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn42.9/en/sujato'>SN 42.9</a>, is the Pāvā known to the Jains. Note, however, that the earliest Jain source for Mahāvīra’s passing, the Kalpasutra, does not say where Pāvā is. But it does say the events were commemorated by the rulers of Kāsī and Kosala, and the Mallians and the Licchavīs. The absence of Magadha and the presence of Malla sit better with the location of Pāvā in Malla rather than Magadha.

## mn56:2.2
- **PLI**: Atha kho dīghatapassī nigaṇṭho nāḷandāyaṁ piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṁ piṇḍapātapaṭikkanto yena pāvārikambavanaṁ yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavatā saddhiṁ sammodi.
- **EN**: Then the Jain ascetic Dīgha Tapassī wandered for alms in Nāḷandā. After the meal, on his return from almsround, he went to Pāvārika’s Mango Grove. There he approached the Buddha, and exchanged greetings with him.
- **NOTE**: In Sanskrit, Dīrghatapasvin was a common name, or rather epithet, for ascetics, meaning “long fervor” in honor of lengthy dedication to practices such as starvation. It is presumably the Dīgha Tapassī of this sutta who is inscribed in a Buddhist stupa at Bharhut teaching a group of students. At least one of his students appears to be holding a small stick, which may be a pictorial representation of the subject of this discourse.

## mn56:3.12  `cck13.57, ms10M_126`
- **PLI**: “Imesaṁ kho, āvuso gotama, tiṇṇaṁ daṇḍānaṁ evaṁ paṭivibhattānaṁ evaṁ paṭivisiṭṭhānaṁ kāyadaṇḍaṁ nigaṇṭho nāṭaputto mahāsāvajjataraṁ paññapeti pāpassa kammassa kiriyāya pāpassa kammassa pavattiyā, no tathā vacīdaṇḍaṁ, no tathā manodaṇḍan”ti.
- **EN**: “The Jain Ñātika describes the physical rod as being the most blameworthy for performing bad deeds, not so much the verbal rod or the mental rod.”
- **NOTE**: In Jainism, all three “rods” create <i lang='pi' translate='no'>karma</i>, which in Jainism is a kind of subtle matter (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>pudgala</i>) that adheres to and obscures the soul (Tattvārthasūtra 6.1–2). Thus all <i lang='pi' translate='no'>karma</i> is material. Nonetheless, according to Dr. Hiralal Jain, <em>Jainism in Buddhist Literature</em>, the Jain position is not that physical actions are more important, since all three kinds of action must be intentional. Rather, it is that intentional actions performed through the body have a greater weight than those performed by speech or by thought alone. <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Karma</i> in the Jain system is the result of such intentional action, which clings to the soul as a form of matter.
