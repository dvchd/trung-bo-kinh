## mn108:1.2
- **PLI**: ekaṁ samayaṁ āyasmā ānando rājagahe viharati veḷuvane kalandakanivāpe aciraparinibbute bhagavati.
- **EN**: At one time Venerable Ānanda was staying near Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrels’ feeding ground. It was not long after the Buddha had become fully quenched.

## mn108:2.1  `nya2`
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena rājā māgadho ajātasattu vedehiputto rājagahaṁ paṭisaṅkhārāpeti rañño pajjotassa āsaṅkamāno.
- **EN**: Now at that time King Ajātasattu of Magadha, son of the princess of Videha, being suspicious of King Pajjota, was having Rājagaha fortified.
- **NOTE**: As to the politics of the situation, Pajjota had established a formidable set of alliances by marriage. He had taken to wife Śivā, daughter of Ceṭaka of the Licchavīs. Prince Bodhi of the Bhaggas was Pajjota’s grandson via his mother Vāsuladattā, queen of the powerful King Udena of Kosambī (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn85/en/sujato#2.1'>MN 85:2.1</a>). Meanwhile, Subāhu of Madhurā had married Pajjota’s sister (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn84/en/sujato#2.1'>MN 84:2.1</a>). Pajjota therefore had allies in Avanti, Bhagga, Vaccha, and Sūrasena, all nations to the west of Magadha, as well as Vajjī, against which Magadha was preparing war to the north. He was also friendly enough with Ajātasattu’s father Bimbisāra to ask him for help in a time of illness (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd8/en/sujato#1.23.4'>Kd 8:1.23.4</a>). The Vinaya relates how Pajjota’s illness was treated by Jīvaka, the doctor of the Buddha and of Bimbisāra. Jīvaka, knowing Pajjota would dislike the medicine, escaped his wrath by fleeing after administering the cure. But when the cure worked, Pajjota favored Jīvaka with a gift of fine cloth. Ajātasattu subsequently murdered Bimbisāra and threatened Pajjota’s allies in Vajjī. Racked by guilt, Ajātasattu saw enemies on all sides; he was even afraid of mendicants meditating in a hall (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn2/en/sujato#10.8'>DN 2:10.8</a>). His fear of Pajjota was not idle, for Pajjota was known for the power of his elephants as much as for his temperamental character. However, we do not hear that any direct conflict flared up at that time. Roughly half a century later, the matter was settled when the Licchavī Śiśunāga, having usurped the throne of Magadha from the Haryaṅka dynasty founded by Bimbisāra, conquered Avanti, Kosala, and Vaccha.

## mn108:8.1  `nya8`
- **PLI**: “Atthi pana, bho ānanda, ekabhikkhupi saṅghena sammato, sambahulehi therehi bhikkhūhi ṭhapito:
- **EN**: “But is there even a single mendicant who has been elected to such a position by the Saṅgha and appointed by several senior mendicants?”
- **NOTE**: The Vinaya sets up procedures whereby the Saṅgha can appoint officials to carry out necessary duties. For example, a monk might be in charge of assigning dwellings (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-ss8/en/sujato#1.1.1'>Bu Ss 8:1.1.1</a>) or teaching the novices (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd16/en/sujato#21.3.30'>Kd 16:21.3.30</a>). Such officials have authority within the roles prescribed to them under the Vinaya as implemented by the Saṅgha. However, they have no special authority outside of that role, and there is no provision for a general role of authority over the Saṅgha as a whole. The Saṅgha followed this precedent at the First Council, where Ānanda was appointed to recite the suttas, and Upāli to recite the Vinaya. Mahākassapa led the proceedings, but decisions were made by the Saṅgha in consensus.

## mn108:9.1  `nya9`
- **PLI**: “Evaṁ appaṭisaraṇe ca pana, bho ānanda, ko hetu sāmaggiyā”ti?
- **EN**: “But since you lack a refuge, worthy Ānanda, what’s the reason for your harmony?”
- **NOTE**: The Buddha had, not long before, taught Vassakāra the eight principles of non-decline by which the Vajjis—as well as the Saṅgha—remained strong due to harmony. There, Vassakāra pointedly said that the Vajjis could only be defeated “by bribery or by sowing dissension” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato#1.5.8'>DN 16:1.5.8</a>). Here he returns to the question of how harmony creates strength. We can imagine that this question was equally relevant in both weakening their enemies as well as strengthening the Magadhans, who cannot have been happy to live under their brutal and patricidal king.
