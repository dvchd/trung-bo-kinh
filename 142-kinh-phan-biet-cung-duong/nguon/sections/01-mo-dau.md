## mn142:5.1  `csp1ed11.298, csp2ed11.298, ms11M_1083, msdiv379, nya5, pts-vp-pli3.255, sya14.459, vri14.305`
- **PLI**: Cuddasa kho panimānanda, pāṭipuggalikā dakkhiṇā.
- **EN**: Ānanda, there are these fourteen religious donations to individuals.
- **NOTE**: “Religious donation” (or “honorarium”, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dakkhiṇā</i>, Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dakṣiṇā</i>) is a Vedic term for the payment owed a brahmin priest for performing ritual services. In the Rig Veda this gift was of cattle offered at the dawn service; it yields benefits as the cow yields milk, and as Dawn sheds her bounteous light. Later it was found that gold or other precious things served just as well. The <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dakṣiṇā</i> was said to absolve the sacrificer from the guilt of killing (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.2.3.4). This is an overriding concern of the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, which emphasizes how the dead of the sacrifice—a slain animal or pressed soma—is “enlivened” through the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dakṣiṇā</i> (2.2.2.2). In the Pali tradition, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dakkhiṇa</i> is etymologically linked with a gift given by the “right” or “capable” hand. In <a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp3.4/en/sujato'>Snp 3.4</a>, a brahmin gives the rice-cake left over from a fire oblation as a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dakkhiṇā</i>.

## mn142:7.9
- **PLI**: Bhikkhunisaṅghe dānaṁ deti—
- **EN**: One gives a gift to the Saṅgha of nuns.
- **NOTE**: If Mahāpajāpati was still a lay person at this point, this mention of the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhikkhunī</i> order could be read in one of two ways. One possibility is that she was not, in fact, the first <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhikkhunī</i>, an argument that I have made previously. In brief, the passages that assert she was the first <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhikkhunī</i>, while widely shared in the tradition, are full of textual problems that indicate they were added at a somewhat late date, which I think was around the time of the Second Council (see <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an8.51/en/sujato'>AN 8.51</a>). Further, the Therīgāthā is the most important early account of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhikkhunīs</i> in their own voices, yet no nun mentions Mahāpajāpati as teacher or founder of the order, while the verses of Bhaddā Kuṇḍalakesā appear to predate the formal ordination procedure ascribed to Mahāpajāpati (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thig5.9/en/sujato'>Thig 5.9</a>). On the other hand, if this argument is incorrect, it might be the case that the order of nuns is mentioned here since the Buddha knew from the start of his dispensation that he would establish it (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato#3.8.4'>DN 16:3.8.4</a>). Under this reading, the Buddha is perhaps dropping a hint for Mahāpajāpati to ordain. Regardless of her ordination status at this point, the praise of the order of nuns acts as an encouragement, balancing out the mild rebuke with which the discourse started (compare the Buddha’s words to Ānanda at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato#5.14.2'>DN 16:5.14.2</a>).

- **EN**: In times to come there will be lambs of the flock wearing a scrap of ocher cloth, unethical and of bad character.
- **NOTE**: In early Pali, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotrabhū</i> is always used for the very least of those who are worthy of gifts (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an9.10/en/sujato#1.3'>AN 9.10:1.3</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an10.16/en/sujato#1.3'>AN 10.16:1.3</a>). It has a positive sense, this negative use being quite isolated. Later Buddhist traditions take <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotrabhū</i> as “one who is joining the clan”, the first entry on the path to awakening, but I doubt this is what it means in early texts. The Buddha recognized clan as a convention (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn98/en/sujato#12.1'>MN 98:12.1</a>), but it was abandoned by those gone forth (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an8.19/en/sujato#14.2'>AN 8.19:14.2</a>), and he considered questions as to his own clan inappropriate (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp3.4/en/sujato#4.3'>Snp 3.4:4.3</a>) as it has nothing to do with purification (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn2.20/en/sujato#3.4'>SN 2.20:3.4</a>). Further, “clan” is always spelled <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotta</i> in Pali. The older Vedic spelling <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotra</i> points to the Vedic meaning of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotra</i> as “cow-pen”, a safe place to gather the herd of cattle, and by extension a name for the herd itself. And while the Saṅgha is never called a “clan” it is compared to a herd of cattle; <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn34/en/sujato'>MN 34</a> develops an extended simile where the different kinds of cattle in the herd are compared with different members of the Saṅgha. Now, in that discourse the last to cross over are the “followers of teachings, followers by faith”. Like a newborn calf, they can only cross the ford with the assistance of their mother (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn34/en/sujato#10.1'>MN 34:10.1</a>); in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an10.16/en/sujato#1.3'>AN 10.16:1.3</a> the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotrabhū</i> follows even them. If we are on the right track, the suffix <i lang='pi' translate='no'>-bhū</i> may be derived from <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhūna</i> (Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhrūṇa</i>) in the sense “baby”, or perhaps “embryo” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn75/en/sujato#5.3'>MN 75:5.3</a>). In English we use “flock” for a religious group under the protection of a leader, so we can render <i lang='pi' translate='no'>gotrabhū</i> as “babies of the flock” or “lambs of the flock”. | For <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kāsāvakaṇṭha</i> as “scrap of ochre cloth”, see note on <a href='https://suttacentral.net/iti48/en/sujato#4.1'>Iti 48:4.1</a>.
