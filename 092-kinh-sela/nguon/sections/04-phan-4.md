## mn92:26.1  `ms10M_1229, nya26`
- **PLI**: “Aggihuttamukhā yaññā,
- **EN**: “The foremost of sacrifices <j>is the offering to the sacred flame;
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Yaññā</i> is a truncated genitive plural (“of sacrifices”). | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Mukha</i> means originally “mouth”, hence “opening”, “entrance”; but also “face”, hence “front”, “foremost”. The fire is the foremost sacrifice, but fire is also a “mouth”, since “however much one may put into a fire, it burns it all up all” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.14.8). This sense of “consume” or “subsume” is alive in these verses as well. For example, the Sāvitrī “subsumes” other hymns, for merely one line is equivalent to all three Vedas (5.14.2).

## mn92:26.2
- **PLI**: sāvittī chandaso mukhaṁ;
- **EN**: the Sāvitrī is the foremost of prayer;
- **NOTE**: The Sāvitrī (or Gāyatrī) is a famous verse recited daily by brahmin priests at dawn: “Might we make our own that desirable effulgence of god Savitar, who will rouse forth our insights” (Rig Veda 3.62.10, Jamison/Brereton). It was the “head” of metres (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>śiro gāyatryaḥ</i>, Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 8.6.2.3, 8.6.2.6), and was “praised as the foremost of recitations” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>gāyatrāṇy uktha mukhanai śaṁsanti</i>, Kauṣītaki Brāhmaṇa 17.7.1). It was the first verse taught a new initiate (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 11.5.4.13). | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Chandas</i> can be either “meter” or a metrical song of praise, especially a Vedic hymn. The Sāvitrī is a Vedic text, not a meter, so the latter meaning must apply here. Its root is “desire”, retaining the sense of “prayer” as a song of praise and supplication.
