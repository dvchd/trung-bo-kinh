## mn124:1.1  `bj12.296, cck14.239, csp1ed11.166, csp2ed11.166, dr13.233, ms11M_559, msdiv209, ndp11.190, nya1, pts-vp-pli3.125, sya14.255, vri14.167`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.
- **NOTE**: Bakkula is depicted as an exponent of austerity who goes significantly beyond the norm in the early texts. According to the commentary, this sutta was added by the Elders at the Second Council, a century after the Buddha’s passing. This late date is supported by multiple textual indications, such as the lack of mention of where the Buddha is staying and the advanced age of Bakkula. Bakkula apparently represents an ideal among a certain wing of the Saṅgha at that time. His verses in the Theragāthā, however, are simple and free from this severity (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thag3.3/en/sujato'>Thag 3.3</a>).

## mn124:1.2
- **PLI**: ekaṁ samayaṁ āyasmā bākulo rājagahe viharati veḷuvane kalandakanivāpe.
- **EN**: At one time Venerable Bakkula was staying near Rājagaha in the Bamboo Grove, the squirrels’ feeding ground.

## mn124:2.1  `nya2`
- **PLI**: Atha kho acelakassapo āyasmato bākulassa purāṇagihisahāyo yenāyasmā bākulo tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā āyasmatā bākulena saddhiṁ sammodi.
- **EN**: Then the naked ascetic Kassapa, an old friend of Bakkula in the lay life, approached him, and exchanged greetings with him.
- **NOTE**: The phrase <i lang='pi' translate='no'>purāṇagihisahāya</i> (“old friend from the lay life”) appears only here and at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn41.9/en/sujato#1.1'>SN 41.9:1.1</a>. There it is also said of a naked ascetic (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>acela</i>) called Kassapa, which cannot be a coincidence. In that case he, as the former friend of the householder Citta, is the one interrogated. The events of the present discourse happened many decades after the Buddha’s death, so it seems likely Kassapa was introduced as a conventional foil for Bakkula. Several such individuals named Kassapa are found in Pali, and their stories are conflated (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn8/en/sujato'>DN 8</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn12.17/en/sujato'>SN 12.17</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn41.9/en/sujato'>SN 41.9</a>).

## mn124:3.9  `ms11M_561, msdiv210`
- **PLI**: “Asīti me, āvuso, vassāni pabbajitassa nābhijānāmi kāmasaññaṁ uppannapubbaṁ”.
- **EN**: “In these eighty years, I don’t recall that any sensual perception has ever arisen in me.”

## mn124:3.10
- **PLI**: “Yampāyasmā bākulo asītiyā vassehi nābhijānāti kāmasaññaṁ uppannapubbaṁ idampi mayaṁ āyasmato bākulassa acchariyaṁ abbhutadhammaṁ dhārema”.
- **EN**: This we remember as an incredible and amazing quality of Venerable Bakkula.
- **NOTE**: According to the commentary, these responses were added by the Elders, representing praise of Bakkula’s austerities by the successful rigorist party at the Second Council. This is supported by several considerations. First, the commentators would rather claim something is early, so when they say it was added later it likely comes from a genuine tradition. Second, the refrains are in plural. Third, the refrains, and the statements of Bakkula that they answer, lack the close <i lang='pi' translate='no'>-ti</i> indicating a direct quote. Fourthly, they continue after the conversation with Kassapa has concluded. The refrains are also found in the Chinese parallel. This shows how comparing different versions of a sutta can take us back only to the point where the texts diverge, which was some time after the Second Council. Thus a shared feature might have been added a century or more after the Buddha.

## mn124:9.1  `bj12.298, ms11M_565, msdiv211, nya9, pts-vp-pli3.126`
- **PLI**: “Asīti me, āvuso, vassāni pabbajitassa nābhijānāmi gahapaticīvaraṁ sāditā”.
- **EN**: “In these eighty years, I don’t recall accepting a robe from a householder …
- **NOTE**: From a discussion of mental purity, Bakkula proceeds to list a series of details of conduct, which are not morally reprehensible, but which illustrate his extreme commitment to austerity. | Accepting a robe from a householder implies enjoying the spoils of wealthy donors. Austere monks wore robes made of cast-off rags. Compare the story of Ānanda at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn52/en/sujato#16.2'>MN 52:16.2</a> = <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an11.16/en/sujato#11.2'>AN 11.16:11.2</a>, or at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd21/en/sujato#1.13.1'>Kd 21:1.13.1</a>, where he explained that after accepting quality robes, they were shared and reused. | This sutta can be seen as representing one of the disputing factions at the Second Council (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd22/en/sujato'>Kd 22</a>). The Saṅgha was divided between “rigorists” who advocated a more austere monastic life, and “laxists” who wanted to take a more relaxed attitude to discipline. The Second Council decided ten points of Vinaya in the rigorists’ favor, the most important of which was whether monastics could use money. Bakkula represents a rigorist position so extreme that if it were followed generally it would undermine the very existence of the Saṅgha. His severity, which even exceeds that of Mahākassapa, is implicitly contrasted with the gentle and accommodating spirit of Ānanda.

## mn124:30-36.4
- **PLI**: nābhijānāmi ābādhaṁ uppannapubbaṁ, antamaso gaddūhanamattampi …
- **EN**: being ill, even for as long as it takes to pull a cow’s udder …
- **NOTE**: Bakkula was said to be the foremost monk in good health (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an1.226/en/sujato#1.1'>AN 1.226:1.1</a>), an accolade that must have been added after his passing away. In later texts, he is often remembered for this (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/tha-ap398/en/sujato'>Tha Ap 398</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mil5.5.3/en/sujato'>Mil 5.5.3</a>; Mahāprajñāpāramitāśāstra 8.2.3.3, Divyāvadāna 253.020). It would seem he mentions it here because many of the other items are allowed for the sake of health. It may happen that something such as a massage, which has a genuine health purpose, can become an indulgence.
