## mn9:16.1
- **PLI**: Katamo cāvuso, dukkhasamudayo?
- **EN**: And what is the origin of suffering?

## mn9:16.2
- **PLI**: Yāyaṁ taṇhā ponobbhavikā nandīrāgasahagatā tatratatrābhinandinī, seyyathidaṁ—
- **EN**: It’s the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights. That is,
- **NOTE**: This definition clarifies two common misunderstandings. First, not all desire causes suffering, for some kinds of desire lead out of suffering (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn51.15/en/sujato#4.1'>SN 51.15:4.1</a>). Second, rebirth is not a tangential part of the Buddha’s teachings, uncritically inherited from cultural suppositions; it is baked into the fundamental meaning of the four noble truths. | The idiom <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tatratatra</i> (literally “there, there”) is not selective (“here and there”) but distributive (“everywhere”). By taking pleasure in sense experience, the mind binds itself to the need for continued stimulation in future lives. The distancing sense “there” is important, for craving does not merely satisfy itself with what it currently experiences, but must always seek out renewed gratification. For an Upaniṣadic precursor, see the note to the same idiom at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn2/en/sujato#8.8'>MN 2:8.8</a>.

## mn9:16.3
- **PLI**: kāmataṇhā bhavataṇhā vibhavataṇhā—
- **EN**: craving for sensual pleasures, craving for continued existence, and craving for nonexistence.
- **NOTE**: Craving is a primal desire or hunger, often unconscious. | “Craving for sensual pleasures” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>kāmataṇhā</i>): the desire to experience pleasure through any of the five senses. | “Craving for continued existence” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhavataṇhā</i>): the desire to continue living in some form after death. | “Craving for nonexistence” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>vibhavataṇhā</i>): the desire to annihilate the self.

## mn9:17.2
- **PLI**: Yo tassāyeva taṇhāya asesavirāganirodho cāgo paṭinissaggo mutti anālayo—
- **EN**: It’s the fading away and cessation of that very same craving with no residue left behind; giving it away, letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it.
- **NOTE**: These terms are used widely in different senses, but here they are all synonyms of “extinction” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>nibbāna</i>). | Note especially the appearance of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>cāga</i> (“giving”) here. Giving is regarded as the most basic foundation of moral practice, yet even in its simplest form it partakes of the same nature as Nibbāna.

- **NOTE**: This is a nice example of Sāriputta’s analytical genius. He picks up “old age and death” from the explanation of “suffering” and treats it within the framework of the four noble truths. This creates continuity with the previous sections, as well as situating it within the Buddha’s teachings as a whole, since all footprints fit in an elephant’s footprint (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn28/en/sujato#2.1'>MN 28:2.1</a>). Meanwhile, this item, following on from “suffering”, leads into the familiar sequence of items from dependent origination. Thus he simultaneously <em>broadens</em> the scope by linking the four noble truths with dependent origination.

## mn9:29.2
- **PLI**: Yato kho, āvuso, ariyasāvako bhavañca pajānāti, bhavasamudayañca pajānāti, bhavanirodhañca pajānāti, bhavanirodhagāminiṁ paṭipadañca pajānāti—
- **EN**: A noble disciple understands continued existence, its origin, its cessation, and the practice that leads to its cessation.
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Bhava</i> means “being, existence, life” in the sense of “past and future lives”. The concept of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhava</i> includes both active and resultant dimensions of life. Consider it by analogy with, say, visiting a park. First there is the spark of an idea, a vague impression of a “park” in the mind, which solidifies into an intention. Acting on it, one goes to the park, where it becomes one’s reality, the “world” one inhabits. Likewise, by creating ideas and volitions in the mind, one is aligning or tuning into the corresponding realm of existence, priming the mind to project itself into that state and make it a reality when one is reborn there.

## mn9:30.2
- **PLI**: Tayome, āvuso, bhavā—
- **EN**: There are these three states of continued existence.
- **NOTE**: The idea of three “worlds” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>loka</i>) or “states of existence” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhava</i>), variously defined, is shared between Buddhism, Hinduism, and Jainism. It originally referred to the earth (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>bhū</i>, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>pṛthivi</i>), the midspace (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>antarikṣa</i>), and the heavens or sky (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>diva</i>, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>svarga</i>), the respective abodes of humanity, the ancestors, and the gods. In the mythology underlying the Vedas they were formed by Indra’s heroic deeds: empowered by soma, he first slew the dragon Vṛtra who bound the world in a mass of darkness, then he separated earth from sky, leaving the midspace between (Rig Veda 2.15), thus creating the visible or intelligible world.
