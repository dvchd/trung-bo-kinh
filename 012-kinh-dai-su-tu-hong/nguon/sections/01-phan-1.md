- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying outside the city of Vesālī in a woodland grove west of the town.

## mn12:2.1  `nya2`
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena sunakkhatto licchaviputto acirapakkanto hoti imasmā dhammavinayā.
- **EN**: Now at that time Sunakkhatta the Licchavi had recently left this teaching and training.
- **NOTE**: Sunakkhatta’s dismal spiritual career began when he met the Buddha in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn105/en/sujato'>MN 105</a>. In <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn6/en/sujato#5.3'>DN 6:5.3</a> we learn that, after being ordained three years, he spoke of his limited success in meditation. The current sutta and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn24/en/sujato'>DN 24</a> deal with Sunakkhatta’s bitter criticisms of the Buddha shortly after his disrobal.

## mn12:2.2
- **PLI**: So vesāliyaṁ parisati evaṁ vācaṁ bhāsati:
- **EN**: He was telling a crowd in Vesālī:

## mn12:2.3
- **PLI**: “natthi samaṇassa gotamassa uttari manussadhammā alamariyañāṇadassanaviseso.
- **EN**: “The ascetic Gotama has no superhuman distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones.
- **NOTE**: These distinctions are defined in the Vinaya as “absorption, release, immersion, attainment, knowledge and vision, development of the path, realization of the fruits, giving up the defilements, a mind without hindrances, delighting in an empty dwelling” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-pj4/en/sujato#3.8'>Bu Pj 4:3.8</a>). They may be summarized as absorption, psychic abilities, and realization of the paths and fruits.

## mn12:2.4
- **PLI**: Takkapariyāhataṁ samaṇo gotamo dhammaṁ deseti vīmaṁsānucaritaṁ sayampaṭibhānaṁ.
- **EN**: He teaches what he’s worked out by logic, following a line of inquiry, expressing his own perspective.
- **NOTE**: The Buddha taught that logic alone is an unreliable guide to the truth (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn76/en/sujato#27.2'>MN 76:27.2</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato#1.34.2'>DN 1:1.34.2</a>), a fact of which Sunakkhatta was apparently not aware.

## mn12:2.5
- **PLI**: Yassa ca khvāssa atthāya dhammo desito so niyyāti takkarassa sammā dukkhakkhayāyā”ti.
- **EN**: Regardless of his goal in teaching the Dhamma, it delivers one who applies it to the complete ending of suffering.”
- **NOTE**: The syntax of this is tricky. The passage typically occurs in contexts where it is not just the efficacy of the teaching in question, but the nature of the goal (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn13/en/sujato#4.2'>DN 13:4.2</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn24/en/sujato#1.4.14'>DN 24:1.4.14</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an3.64/en/sujato#10.2'>AN 3.64:10.2</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an4.8/en/sujato#4.1'>AN 4.8:4.1</a>). Here the opening pronouns coordinate, both in genitive: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>yassa ca khv’assa</i>. This shows that where, later at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn12/en/sujato#26.1'>MN 12:26.1</a>, we find <i lang='pi' translate='no'>yassa kho pana te</i>, the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>te</i> is also genitive (not instrumental as might be assumed; but see <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn24/en/sujato#1.4.14'>DN 24:1.4.14</a>). Thus the point of this expression is “whatever the goal is <em>for you</em>”. Rhetorically, Sunakkhatta is saying that Gotama’s intended goal is irrelevant. | <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Niyyāti</i> means “emancipates”, “delivers” (rather than “leads to”), and relates to the view that is “emancipating” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>niyyānika</i>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn12/en/sujato#56.3'>MN 12:56.3</a>). It need not, however, mean “liberation” in the Buddhist sense, for it is also used of the Brahmanical goal (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn13/en/sujato#4.2'>DN 13:4.2</a>). | Further, the expression “for the complete ending of suffering” refers to the one who is practicing for such a goal (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn12.51/en/sujato#2.1'>SN 12.51:2.1</a>). Thus the view or the teaching is emancipating “for one who applies it to the complete ending of suffering”. | See Bodhi, <em>Numerical Discourses</em>, note 451 for the commentarial reading, which differs in some respects from mine. He gives some Chinese parallels; another at T 757 raises the question, “How could such a teaching lead to freedom from suffering?”
