## mn22:10.5
- **PLI**: Te upārambhānisaṁsā ceva dhammaṁ pariyāpuṇanti itivādappamokkhānisaṁsā ca.
- **EN**: They memorize the teaching for the sake of finding fault and winning debates.
- **NOTE**: This theme is expanded in several discourses of the Aṭṭhakavagga, such as <a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp4.8/en/sujato'>Snp 4.8</a>.

## mn22:10.6
- **PLI**: Yassa catthāya dhammaṁ pariyāpuṇanti tañcassa atthaṁ nānubhonti.
- **EN**: They don’t realize the goal for which they memorized them.

## mn22:10.7
- **PLI**: Tesaṁ te dhammā duggahitā dīgharattaṁ ahitāya dukkhāya saṁvattanti.
- **EN**: Because they’re wrongly grasped, those teachings lead to their lasting harm and suffering.

## mn22:10.8
- **PLI**: Taṁ kissa hetu?
- **EN**: Why is that?

## mn22:10.9
- **PLI**: Duggahitattā, bhikkhave, dhammānaṁ.
- **EN**: Because of their wrong grasp of the teachings.
- **NOTE**: Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.10 says that those who worship ignorance enter darkness, but those who love knowledge (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>veda</i>) enter even greater darkness. This is a recurring theme; for example, Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.1.2 tells how Uddālaka saw the vanity of his son Śvetaketu when he returned from his schooling.

## mn22:10.10  `cck12.253, dr12.240, ms9M_787, pts-vp-pli1.134`
- **PLI**: Seyyathāpi, bhikkhave, puriso alagaddatthiko alagaddagavesī alagaddapariyesanaṁ caramāno.
- **EN**: Suppose there was a person in need of a cobra. And while wandering in search of a cobra
- **NOTE**: The <i lang='pi' translate='no'>alagadda</i> appears only here in early Pali. Sanskrit sources identify <i lang='pi' translate='no'>alagarda</i> either as a water-snake—in which case, however, it is said to be non-venomous (Kṣīrasvāmin’s Amarakoṣodghāṭana 1.7.5)—or as a kind of cobra (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>darvīkara</i>, Suśrutasaṁhitā 5.4), defined as such by its hood. The <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sappa</i> is also said to be hooded, so I translate both words as “cobra”. | As to why the man was looking for a cobra, the commentary says he was looking to harvest the snake’s venom. But Candrakīrti, drawing on the pan-Indic legend that certain serpents have a “snake-gem” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>nāgamaṇi</i>) in their heads, says that a serpent captured with the proper herbs and incantations brings great riches, but should these fail it will turn deadly (Mūlamadhyamakavṛtti-prasannapadā, L. de La Vallée Poussin’s translation, page 497).

## mn22:13.1  `ms9M_790, msdiv240, nya13`
