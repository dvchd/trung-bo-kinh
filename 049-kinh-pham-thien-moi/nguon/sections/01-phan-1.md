## mn49:1.1  `bj10.764, dr12.519, ms9M_1711, msdiv501, ndp9.399, nya1, pts-vp-pli1.326, sya12.590, vri12.409`

- **NOTE**: This sutta is a unique fusion of high philosophy and cosmic drama, positing an unthinkable alliance between what in Christian terms would be called God and the Devil. Nonetheless, due to a plethora of textual difficulties and uncertain readings, caution in interpretation would be wise.

## mn49:2.1  `cck12.562, ms9M_1712, nya2`
- **PLI**: “Ekamidāhaṁ, bhikkhave, samayaṁ ukkaṭṭhāyaṁ viharāmi subhagavane sālarājamūle.
- **EN**: “This one time, mendicants, I was staying near Ukkaṭṭhā in the Subhaga Forest at the root of a magnificent sal tree.
- **NOTE**: <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn6.4/en/sujato'>SN 6.4</a>, which otherwise opens with the same events, is simply set in Sāvatthī with no mention of Ukkaṭṭhā, which is the same setting as <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn1/en/sujato'>MN 1</a>, with which this sutta shares some themes. Both critique the sophisticated philosophy of the Kosalan brahmins, and hence are set in the town of their leader Pokkharasāti (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn3/en/sujato#1.2.1'>DN 3:1.2.1</a>).

## mn49:2.2
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana, bhikkhave, samayena bakassa brahmuno evarūpaṁ pāpakaṁ diṭṭhigataṁ uppannaṁ hoti:
- **EN**: Now at that time Baka the Divinity had the following harmful misconception:
- **NOTE**: Baka maintains the same wrong view at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn6.4/en/sujato'>SN 6.4</a>, where his past lives are revealed. The word <i lang='pi' translate='no'>baka</i> means “stork” or “crane”. To search for a high divinity of this name in Brahmanism is to be disappointed, for instead we find a man-eating demon (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>rakṣasa</i>) in bird form whose fate is to be slain by the hero Bhīma (or Kṛṣṇa). Pali stories tell of how the stork dozes peacefully as if meditating by the water, while in reality he is trying to fool fish into approaching so he can snatch them up (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja38/en/sujato'>Ja 38</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja236/en/sujato'>Ja 236</a>). A cunning, large, white, high-flying, predatory bird who fakes meditation is a fitting image for the antagonist of these suttas.

## mn49:2.3
- **PLI**: ‘idaṁ niccaṁ, idaṁ dhuvaṁ, idaṁ sassataṁ, idaṁ kevalaṁ, idaṁ acavanadhammaṁ, idañhi na jāyati na jīyati na mīyati na cavati na upapajjati, ito ca panaññaṁ uttari nissaraṇaṁ natthī’ti.
- **EN**: ‘This is permanent, this is everlasting, this is eternal, this is whole, this is not liable to pass away. For this is where there’s no being born, growing old, dying, passing away, or being reborn. And there’s no other escape beyond this.’
- **NOTE**: The use of the impersonal pronoun (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>idaṁ</i>) to refer to the self as divinity is a characteristic Upaniṣadic idiom: “you are that” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>tat tvam asi</i>, Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.8.7); “that self is divinity” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>ayam ātmā brahma</i>, Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 1.2); “I am that” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>so’ham asmi</i>, Īśa Upaniṣad 16); “this is that self hidden in all” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>eṣa ta ātmā sarvāntaraḥ</i>, Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.4.1, etc.); “this, verily, is that” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>etad vai tat</i>, Kaṭha Upaniṣad 2.1.5). | For <i lang='pi' translate='no'>idaṁ niccaṁ</i> see <i lang='pi' translate='no'>eṣa nityo</i> (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.23); for <i lang='pi' translate='no'>idaṁ dhuvaṁ</i> see <i lang='pi' translate='no'>etad apramayaṁ dhruvam</i> (4.4.20).

## mn49:5.3

## mn49:5.8
- **PLI**: “iṅgha tvaṁ, mārisa, yadeva te brahmā āha tadeva tvaṁ karohi, mā tvaṁ brahmuno vacanaṁ upātivattittho”.
- **EN**: please, good fellow, do exactly what the Divinity says. Don’t go beyond the word of the Divinity.

## mn49:5.9
- **PLI**: Sace kho tvaṁ, bhikkhu, brahmuno vacanaṁ upātivattissasi, seyyathāpi nāma puriso siriṁ āgacchantiṁ daṇḍena paṭippaṇāmeyya, seyyathāpi vā pana, bhikkhu, puriso narakappapāte papatanto hatthehi ca pādehi ca pathaviṁ virādheyya, evaṁ sampadamidaṁ, bhikkhu, tuyhaṁ bhavissati.
- **EN**: If you do, then you’ll end up like a person who, when approached by Lady Luck, would fend her off with a staff; or who, as they are falling over a cliff, would push away the ground with their hands and feet.
- **NOTE**: “Lady Luck” is Siri, later famed as goddess of fortune and prosperity. The same idiom recurs at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/thag8.3/en/sujato#2.1'>Thag 8.3:2.1</a>. As to why someone would “fend her off” (see note on <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn37/en/sujato#7.3'>MN 37:7.3</a>), Siri (Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>śrī</i>) is identified with Lakṣmī “the undeparting” (Śrī Sūkta 2, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>lakṣmīmanapagāminīm</i>). Atharva Veda 7.115 opens with, “Fly away wicked Lakṣmī, vanish, fly hence”. Apparently 101 Lakṣmīs attach to a man when born, and the spell chases away the wicked (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>pāpi</i>) while keeping the good (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>puṇya</i>). In this case the Pali text does not draw directly from the Veda, as there is no close verbal parallel, and the Pali presents as irrational an action that makes good sense in the context of the Vedic passage. Rather, it would seem, the Pali draws from a more general cultural awareness. | For “falling over a cliff” see <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn12/en/sujato#78.2'>DN 12:78.2</a>.
