## mn26:1.1  `bj10.394, cck12.297, csp1ed9.216, csp2ed9.216, ms9M_933, msdiv272, ndp9.209, nya1, sya12.312, vri12.219`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.
- **NOTE**: This discourse is known in Pali manuscripts and commentaries either as “The Noble Quest” or “The Pile of Snares”, whereas the Chinese parallel at MA 204 is titled “The Discourse at Rāma’s Hermitage”. It is one of several discourses in the Majjhima that include a partial account of Siddhattha’s practice before awakening, an account that became one of the key events in the Buddha’s biography. This particular version focuses on his experience with Brahmanical teachers, while <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn36/en/sujato'>MN 36</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn85/en/sujato'>MN 85</a>, and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn100/en/sujato'>MN 100</a> include a long passage detailing his Jain-like fervent austerities.

- **EN**: Then several mendicants went up to Venerable Ānanda and said to him,

## mn26:2.3
- **PLI**: “cirassutā no, āvuso ānanda, bhagavato sammukhā dhammī kathā.
- **EN**: “Reverend, it’s been a long time since we’ve heard a Dhamma talk from the Buddha.
- **NOTE**: Why was the Buddha reluctant to teach? This is puzzling because where we find this trope elsewhere there is a clear reason: at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an7.52/en/sujato#1.3'>AN 7.52:1.3</a> the Buddha was making one of his rare visits to Campā, while at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn22.81/en/sujato#3.5'>SN 22.81:3.5</a> he had left the squabbling Saṅgha for the seclusion of the forest. Here, in the central teaching location of Sāvatthī, there is no such reason. Rather, the events are best understood as narrative foreshadowing. His reluctance requires an intervention by others, and when the teaching finally takes place it is not in a Buddhist monastery but on Brahmanical grounds. The story goes on to tell how, after his awakening, he was reluctant to teach until the intervention of Brahmā (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn26/en/sujato#19.1'>MN 26:19.1</a>). This narrative mirroring creates a hidden link (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sandhi</i>) that frames the awakening in a Brahmanical context. The very obscurity of the connection is the point, for “the gods love hidden things”. The theme of reluctance is extended further to the reluctance of the five mendicants to receive the Buddha. It draws on the precedent of Yājñavalkya’s reluctance to teach Janaka (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.1).

## mn26:5.2
- **PLI**: ariyā ca pariyesanā, anariyā ca pariyesanā.
- **EN**: the noble quest and the ignoble quest.
- **NOTE**: These opposing quests (or “searches”, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>pariyesanā</i>) respond to Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.22, which we have already noted as a source for the phrase <i lang='pi' translate='no'>so attā so loko</i> (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn22/en/sujato#15.10'>MN 22:15.10</a>). The inner self sought by the brahmins is described as unborn, made of consciousness, undecaying, unattached, and unaffected by karma; knowing it one becomes a sage. See too BU 3.5.1.

## mn26:6.1  `ms9M_940, ndp9.211, nya6`
- **PLI**: Kiñca, bhikkhave, jātidhammaṁ vadetha?
- **EN**: And what should be described as liable to be reborn?
- **NOTE**: The Pali includes “gold and money” among those things subject to birth, old age, and defilement. The Chinese parallel MA 204 (T i 776a7; cf. T 765 at T xvii 679b23) does not mention “birth” but says money and jewelry are subject to old age, defilement, etc. Elsewhere in the suttas, rebirth, old age, and defilement are qualities of sentient beings, not inanimate objects, so this might be a textual corruption. Nonetheless, the commentary offers explanations for why gold and money are included under these specific categories and not the rest, and this, together with the Chinese text, shows that if it is a corruption it is an old one. I give the commentarial explanations.

## mn26:6.2
- **PLI**: Puttabhariyaṁ, bhikkhave, jātidhammaṁ, dāsidāsaṁ jātidhammaṁ, ajeḷakaṁ jātidhammaṁ, kukkuṭasūkaraṁ jātidhammaṁ, hatthigavāssavaḷavaṁ jātidhammaṁ, jātarūparajataṁ jātidhammaṁ.
- **EN**: Partners and children, male and female bondservants, goats and sheep, chickens and pigs, elephants and cattle, and gold and currency are liable to be reborn.
- **NOTE**: Gold and money are subject to “birth” because they are produced by heat (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>utusamuṭṭhāna</i>). | The word for “gold” here, one of several in Pali, is <i lang='pi' translate='no'>jātarūpa</i>, literally “born form”, i.e. that which is naturally beautiful. In Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 6.4.25, a newborn infant is to be fed mixed curd, honey, and ghee with a piece of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>jātarūpa</i>, thus placing “golden” Vedic speech in him. This rare early instance of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>jātarūpa</i> shows that it had a felt connection with the idea of birth. Perhaps this connection prompted its inclusion here. From that, “money” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>rajata</i>) was brought in as the two form a stock phrase, and they were then applied elsewhere in the list.
