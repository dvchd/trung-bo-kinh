## mn7:17.1  `bj10.90, dr12.61, ms9M_184, msdiv78, ndp9.52, nya17, pts-vp-pli1.39`
- **PLI**: So ‘atthi idaṁ, atthi hīnaṁ, atthi paṇītaṁ, atthi imassa saññāgatassa uttari nissaraṇan’ti pajānāti.
- **EN**: They understand: ‘There is this, there is what is worse than this, there is what is better than this, and there is an escape beyond the scope of perception.’
- **NOTE**: This is a description of advanced insight by a stream-enterer. The meditator understands the current state of their experience, namely the mind developed through <i lang='pi' translate='no'>samādhi</i>. They know that this is conditioned and hence liable to decline to a lower state “worse than this”. They know that there are still higher states of mind that can be developed. And they know that, while all states of meditation fall within the scope of perception, there is an ultimate escape, namely Nibbāna.

- **PLI**: “Lokkhasammatā hi, bho gotama, bāhukā nadī bahujanassa, puññasammatā hi, bho gotama, bāhukā nadī bahujanassa, bāhukāya pana nadiyā bahujano pāpakammaṁ kataṁ pavāhetī”ti.
- **EN**: “Many people deem that the Bāhukā River leads to a heavenly world and bestows merit. And many people rinse off their bad deeds in the Bāhukā River.”
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Lokkha</i>, elsewhere unattested in Pali, is Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>lokya</i>, which occurs several times in the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa in the sense “conducive to a (heavenly) world” (9.5.2.16, 10.5.2.12, 11.3.3.7). The root sense of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>loka</i> is “light” and it was originally the bright sky with its <i lang='pi' translate='no'>devas</i> (eg. Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa Mādyandina 11.2.3.1–6, Kāṇva 3.2.5.1), later being extended to include all worlds. The PTS reading <i lang='pi' translate='no'>mokkha</i> is an unwarranted normalization, although it is supported by the parallel at MA 93, which has <span lang='lzh'>度</span>. Note that the expected form <i lang='pi' translate='no'>lokiya</i>, although common in later Pali, occurs only in one early verse (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thag2.18/en/sujato#2.4'>Thag 2.18:2.4</a>). | Bathing for purity from misdeeds as a pre-Buddhist Brahmanical custom is attested in Śukla Yajurveda 3.48. The bhikkhunī Puṇṇikā pointed out that if this were true then the fish, frogs, and turtles would go to heaven (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thig12.1/en/sujato#6.1'>Thig 12.1:6.1</a>); her argument was echoed by the Jains (Sūyagaḍa 1.7.14–16) and later Brahmins (Liṅga-purāṇa 1.8.33–4).

## mn7:20.3
- **PLI**: gayaṁ sundarikaṁ mapi;
- **EN**: at Gayā and the Sundarikā too,
- **NOTE**: Gayā is a ford on the Phalgu River by the town of Gayā, which is still a popular site for sacred bathing. Legend has it that it was named after the great titan of that name who offered his own body in sacrifice (Vāyu Purāṇa vol. ii, 43.5, Tagare translation). | Sundarikā was a river in Kosala used for Brahmanical rituals (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn7.9/en/sujato'>SN 7.9</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp3.4/en/sujato'>Snp 3.4</a>). Bathing in the ford there was said to bring beauty (Mahābhārata 3.82.51).

## mn7:20.4
- **PLI**: Sarassatiṁ payāgañca,
- **EN**: Sarasvatī and Payāga,
- **NOTE**: Pali <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sarassatī</i> is better known in the Sanskrit form <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sarasvatī</i>, the most prominent river of the Vedas, who “like a snorting boar, broke the back of the mountains with her mighty waves” (Rig Veda 6.61.2). Change stole her waters and today she is lost. Researchers have identified her with the now-residual Ghaggar-Hakra system in north-west India and Pakistan or the Helmand River in Afghanistan. | Payāga (modern Prayagraj, formerly Allahabad) is the sacred ford at the confluence of the Ganges and the Yamunā beside Kosambī (see <a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-pj1/en/sujato#4.18'>Bu Pj 1:4.18</a>). The Mahābhārata calls it the “vulva of the world” (3.83.71), the most meritorious of all fords, where bathing can wash away a hundred crimes (3.83.82).
