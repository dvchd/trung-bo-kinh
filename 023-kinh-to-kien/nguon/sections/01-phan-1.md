- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying near Sāvatthī in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s monastery.

## mn23:1.3
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena āyasmā kumārakassapo andhavane viharati.
- **EN**: Now at that time Venerable Kassapa the Prince was staying in the Dark Forest.
- **NOTE**: “Kassapa the Prince” (Kumārakassapa) was ordained at twenty (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd1/en/sujato#75.1.1'>Kd 1:75.1.1</a>). His verses are collected in the Theragāthā (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thag2.41/en/sujato'>Thag 2.41</a>). His love of elaborate similes and playful similes is shown in the Pāyāsisutta (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn23/en/sujato'>DN 23</a>), and echoed here in the teachings given to him. He was declared the foremost of those with brilliant speech (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an1.217/en/sujato'>AN 1.217</a>). | The Dark Forest (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>andhavana</i>) was a thick grove south of Sāvatthī often visited by monks and nuns for meditation. However, this is the only discourse where someone is said to be staying there. The commentary says the name means “Blind Man’s Forest”, giving an improbable origin story. But compare Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>andhakūpa</i>, “dark well” or “blind well”, a well covered over with a lid or overgrowth; also the name of a hell.We might translate “Mirkwood”.

## mn23:1.4
- **PLI**: Atha kho aññatarā devatā abhikkantāya rattiyā abhikkantavaṇṇā kevalakappaṁ andhavanaṁ obhāsetvā yenāyasmā kumārakassapo tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā ekamantaṁ aṭṭhāsi. Ekamantaṁ ṭhitā kho sā devatā āyasmantaṁ kumārakassapaṁ etadavoca:
- **EN**: Then, late at night, a glorious deity, lighting up the entire Dark Forest, went up to Kassapa the Prince, stood to one side, and said:
- **NOTE**: The deity offers a series of obscure riddles full of secret meaning, almost like a dream sequence. The deliberate use of obscurity is a hallmark of Brahmanical literature, for “the gods love hidden things” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>parokṣakāmā hi devāḥ</i>, Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 10.6.2.2 and throughout). The sequence is set out as a quest for buried treasure. This mytheme is implicit in the Vedic myth of Indra freeing the cattle and the sun from the Vala cave, an act that liberates the truth. Sometimes it is made explicit, as when Indra is said to bring up the treasures buried deep (Rig Veda 8.66.4). The Aśvins are also associated with unearthing buried gems (Rig Veda 1.117.5) or gold (Rig Veda 1.117.12), imagery ultimately based in mining for the wealth of minerals underground.

## mn23:2.1  `bj10.356, cck12.268, csp1ed9.196, csp2ed9.196, dr12.253, ms9M_838, nya2, pts-vp-pli1.143, sya12.283`
- **PLI**: “Bhikkhu bhikkhu, ayaṁ vammiko rattiṁ dhūmāyati, divā pajjalati.
- **EN**: “Monk, monk! This termite mound fumes by night and flames by day.
- **NOTE**: Worship of termite mounds and the deadly snakes they harbor is still common in India today and is probably pre-Vedic. The Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa acknowledges a ritual significance to the anthill as a creative manifestation of the earth upon which offerings may be laid and whose ants were divine (2.6.2.17, 6.3.3.5, 14.1.2.10). In Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.7, Yājñavalkya compares a dead body bereft of soul to the lifeless slough of a snake on an anthill. | While anthills don’t “fume” and “flame”, large termite mounds leverage the temperature differential of day and night to create convection flows that regulate temperature and flush carbon dioxide.

## mn23:2.3
- **PLI**: ‘abhikkhaṇa, sumedha, satthaṁ ādāyā’ti.
- **EN**: ‘Dig, clever one, having picked up the sword!’
- **NOTE**: The sword is taken up, a symbol whose roots lie in the discovery of metal smelting and the power this grants to the one who wields the sword. But the phrase has a double meaning, for <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sattha</i> means both “sword” and “sacred treatise”, while <i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhikkhaṇa</i> means both “dig” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhi</i> + <i lang='pi' translate='no'>√khaṇ</i>) and “see” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhi</i> + <i lang='pi' translate='no'>√ikkh</i>; this sense not in Pali, but cf. Vedic <i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhikhyā</i>). Thus, having entered the Dark Wood, the “clever one” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sumedha</i>) is urged to use the wisdom of scripture in order to see. This sense is reinforced by the fact that it is the “brahmin” who says this.

## mn23:2.4
- **PLI**: Abhikkhaṇanto sumedho satthaṁ ādāya addasa laṅgiṁ
- **EN**: Picking up the sword and digging, the clever one saw a sticking point:
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Laṅgi</i> occurs only here, and is glossed by the commentary with <i lang='pi' translate='no'>paligha</i> (“obstacle”). Such an obscure term must have been selected for a play on words. Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>lagna</i> has the primary sense “to stick, adhere”, while also having a sense of the beginning or coming into contact with something. In modern languages such as Hindi, Marathi, or Kannada it has taken on the sense of “marriage”, and perhaps here we see an anticipation of this. If we are on the right track, the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>laṅgi</i> would be the initial challenge for a mendicant seeker, namely their attachment to family. This would explain why it is the first obstacle.

- **EN**: Picking up the sword and digging, the clever one saw a forked path:
- **NOTE**: Rig Veda 10.88.15 speaks of the two paths of gods and mortals (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>devānām uta martyānām</i>), later formalized as the path of the forefathers (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>pitṛyāna</i>) leading to rebirth and that of the gods (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>devayāna</i>) leading to liberation. This corresponds to the twofold choice of the young Siddhattha: to stay at home or to go forth. It might also be the paths open to the Bodhisatta under his Brahmanical teachers: to seek eternal existence or an escape from rebirth.

- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Caṅgavāra</i> is a filter through which, according to <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja525/en/sujato#29.2'>Ja 525:29.2</a>, water drips away like the brief days of our lives. The commentaries say that such alkaline (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>khāra</i>) filters were used by laundrymen. This refers to the traditional method of creating lye (a soap precursor) for washing, where a barrel is filled with wood ash through which water is passed. No matter how many pots of water are poured in, the commentary adds, it keeps dripping.

- **NOTE**: As described in Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 7.5.1, a tortoise was placed on the altar to represent the primordial powers of creation: the sap of life, the sun, the breath, and the act of creation itself. ## mn23:2.29
- **PLI**: Abhikkhaṇanto sumedho satthaṁ ādāya addasa asisūnaṁ.
- **EN**: Picking up the sword and digging, the clever one saw a butcher’s knife and chopping board:
- **NOTE**: In this brutal simile, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>asi</i> is a knife and <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sūna</i> is a place where animals are slaughtered, either a slaughterhouse or, as here, an implement for the slaughter. <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mnd1/en/sujato#23.18'>Mnd 1:23.18</a> says this phrase has the meaning of “chopping” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>adhikuṭṭanaṭṭhena</i>). Compare with <i lang='pi' translate='no'>asiṁ sūnāṁ</i> in the violent, sexually graphic hymn at Rig Veda 10.86.18c. Vedic <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sūna</i> was a (sewn) “basket” for carrying the flesh of a slaughtered beast (see also Rig Veda 1.161.10), a sense that developed to “chopping block” and “slaughterhouse”. Jamison and Brereton read the Vedic passage as an analogy for the horse sacrifice, at the climax of which the queen has vulgar and very public sex with the dead horse. This cements in the most explicit way possible the oneness of sexual potency with the cycle of life and death.
