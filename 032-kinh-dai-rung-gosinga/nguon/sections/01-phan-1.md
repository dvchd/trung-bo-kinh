## mn32:6.7
- **PLI**: Seyyathāpi, āvuso sāriputta, cakkhumā puriso uparipāsādavaragato sahassaṁ nemimaṇḍalānaṁ volokeyya;
- **EN**: just as a person with clear eyes could survey a thousand orbits from the upper floor of a royal longhouse.
- **NOTE**: This power is compared (in the Majjhima) with a man standing between two doors watching people come to and fro (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn39/en/sujato#20.3'>MN 39:20.3</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn77/en/sujato#35.2'>MN 77:35.2</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn130/en/sujato#2.1'>MN 130:2.1</a>), and (in the Dīgha) with someone in a longhouse watching people move about in the town square (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn2/en/sujato#96.1'>DN 2:96.1</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn10/en/sujato#2.33.1'>DN 10:2.33.1</a>). The unique phrasing here evokes the Wheel that manifests to a Wheel-Turning Monarch as he stands upstairs in the royal longhouse, which is thousand-spoked and complete with rim (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sahassāraṁ sanemikaṁ</i>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn129/en/sujato#34.2'>MN 129:34.2</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn26/en/sujato#4.9'>DN 26:4.9</a>). The simile was evidently unclear to the ancients, as one Chinese parallel has instead “a thousand bricks” (presumably laid out to dry in the sun, MA 184 at T i 727b14). In the Pali tradition, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nemimaṇḍala</i> is the outer cladding of a wheel rim, often iron (see commentary to <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja475/en/sujato'>Ja 475</a>). However, normally sutta similes draw directly from experience, and it is hard to imagine how one would survey a thousand wheel rims (unless perhaps a king overseeing chariot construction in his forecourt?) On the other hand, the Wheel is a solar symbol and <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nemimaṇḍala</i> could easily refer to the orbits of the stars, which would explain “looking at the sky” of EA 37.3 (at T ii 711a3). On balance, I think the simile refers to a star-gazing king.
