## mn74:1.1  `bj11.280, cck13.267, csp1ed10.166, csp2ed10.166, dr13.242, mc2.179, mc2.180, ms10M_588, msdiv201, ndp10.193, ndp10.194, nya1, pts-vp-pli1.498, sya13.263, sya13.264, vri13.176`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.
- **NOTE**: This sutta records the occasion of the full enlightenment of the Buddha’s greatest disciple, Sāriputta, as well as the conversion of Dīghanakha. The commentary says this became the occasion for a unique four-factored gathering: it is the full moon in the month of Māgha; 1,250 mendicants spontaneously assemble; all were arahants with the six high knowledges; all were ordained by the “come, mendicant” formula. Today this is celebrated in the Makha Puja festival in Thailand.

## mn74:1.2

- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying near Rājagaha, on the Vulture’s Peak Mountain in the Boar’s Cave.

## mn74:2.1  `nya2`
- **PLI**: Atha kho dīghanakho paribbājako yena bhagavā tenupasaṅkami; upasaṅkamitvā bhagavatā saddhiṁ sammodi.
- **EN**: Then the wanderer Dīghanakha went up to the Buddha, and exchanged greetings with him.
- **NOTE**: Dīghanakha means “Long-nails”; it must have been a nickname. Long nails are forbidden in the Vinaya (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd15/en/sujato#27.1.21'>Kd 15:27.1.21</a>) and also to the one performing a Brahmanical consecration (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>dīkṣā</i>, Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 3.1.2). Further, EA 34.4 says that long nails (<span lang='lzh'>爪長</span>), uncut hair, and unwashed robes were disgraceful characteristics of ascetics. Ācārāṅgasūtra 2.8.5 speaks of the ascetic practice of abandoning the care of the hair of the head, beard, and nails. | Dīghanakha was Sāriputta’s nephew according to the Pali commentaries (not here, but in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn111/en/sujato'>MN 111</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn11.2/en/sujato'>SN 11.2</a>, etc.) and northern texts such as Mahāprajñāpāramitā-upadeśa-śāstra, Mahāvibhāṣā, and Avadānaśataka.

## mn74:2.4
- **PLI**: ‘sabbaṁ me nakkhamatī’”ti.
- **EN**: ‘Nothing is acceptable to me.’”
- **NOTE**: Dīghanakha’s view “nothing is acceptable to me” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sabbaṁ me nakkhamati</i>) is obscure, which seems to be the point. Sanskrit and Tibetan parallels to this passage are identical to the Pali, so when some Chinese translations say “no view” or “no teaching” is acceptable, this is probably because the translators felt the need to clarify, echoing the common expression, “the acceptance of a view after consideration” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>diṭṭhinijjhānakkhanti</i>). This reading fits the text, but the sense may be broader than that. The closest phrasing in Pali is at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn25/en/sujato#10.11'>DN 25:10.11</a>, where “this is not acceptable to me” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>idaṁ me nakkhamati</i>) refers to food that a picky monk rejects. Views about acceptable food are recorded in detail in Jain texts, and we may presume that other orders had similar systems. Strictness in such matters is seen as a sign of virtue, escalating ultimately to the refusal of all food.

## mn74:2.5
- **PLI**: “Yāpi kho te esā, aggivessana, diṭṭhi:
- **EN**: “This view of yours, Aggivessana—
- **NOTE**: Aggivessana is his Brahmanical clan name.

## mn74:2.6
- **PLI**: ‘sabbaṁ me nakkhamatī’ti, esāpi te diṭṭhi nakkhamatī”ti?
- **EN**: is that acceptable to you?”
- **NOTE**: While this displays the Buddha’s ready wit, like much good humor it plays on a deeper anxiety. For it is easy to say that one believes in nothing, but the state of unknowing is psychologically unstable, as minds seek answers. When someone claims to hold no views, their skepticism is how they hide their views from themselves.
