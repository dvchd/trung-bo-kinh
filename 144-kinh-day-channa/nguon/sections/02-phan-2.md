## mn144:11.1  `cck14.436, ms11M_1141, msdiv393, nya11, sya14.479`
- **PLI**: Evaṁ vutte, āyasmā mahācundo āyasmantaṁ channaṁ etadavoca:
- **EN**: When he said this, Venerable Mahācunda said to Venerable Channa:

## mn144:11.2
- **PLI**: “tasmātiha, āvuso channa, idampi tassa bhagavato sāsanaṁ, niccakappaṁ manasi kātabbaṁ:
- **EN**: “So, Reverend Channa, you should regularly apply your mind to this instruction of the Buddha:
- **NOTE**: Mahācunda is about to quote a phrase that appears at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ud8.4/en/sujato'>Ud 8.4</a>.

## mn144:11.3
- **PLI**: ‘nissitassa calitaṁ, anissitassa calitaṁ natthi.
- **EN**: ‘For the dependent there is quivering. For the independent there’s no quivering.
- **NOTE**: The “dependent” is the world of conditions. The “independent” is Nibbāna. | “Quivering” is <i lang='pi' translate='no'>calita</i>.

## mn144:11.4
- **PLI**: Calite asati passaddhi,
- **EN**: When there’s no quivering, there is tranquility.

## mn144:11.5
- **PLI**: passaddhiyā sati nati na hoti.
- **EN**: When there is tranquility, there’s no inclination.
- **NOTE**: “Inclination” is <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nati</i>, from a root meaning “to bend”. Sometimes used in the sense of a “inclination of mind” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn19/en/sujato#6.1'>MN 19:6.1</a>), here, as the following lines make clear, it refers to the mind inclining to rebirth; explained by the commentary as “craving”. Compare the English idiom, “one is bent on destruction”.

## mn144:11.6
- **PLI**: Natiyā asati āgatigati na hoti.
- **EN**: When there’s no inclination, there’s no coming and going.

## mn144:11.7
- **PLI**: Āgatigatiyā asati cutūpapāto na hoti.
- **EN**: When there’s no coming and going, there’s no passing away and reappearing.

## mn144:11.8
- **PLI**: Cutūpapāte asati nevidha na huraṁ na ubhayamantarena.
- **EN**: When there’s no passing away and reappearing, there’s no this life or the next or between the two.
- **NOTE**: Vedic cosmology speaks of a tripartite world: the earth, the heavens, and the “midspace” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>antarikṣa</i>), namely the atmosphere. The gods journey through the midspace to earth for the sacrifice, bringing bounty for mortals (eg. Rig Veda 7.45). Later, the process of rebirth was conceived of as a path of cosmological ascent returning to the world of the gods or the fathers (eg. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 5.10.1). For the suttas, rebirth is likewise seen as a process of moving between states, like a person leaving a house, walking down the street, and entering another house (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn2/en/sujato#96.1'>DN 2:96.1</a>).

## mn144:11.9
- **PLI**: Esevanto dukkhassā’”ti.
- **EN**: Just this is the end of suffering.’”

## mn144:13.8
- **PLI**: Nāhaṁ, sāriputta, ettāvatā ‘saupavajjo’ti vadāmi.
- **EN**: But, Sāriputta, this is not enough for me to call someone ‘blameworthy’.

## mn144:13.9
- **PLI**: Yo kho, sāriputta, imañca kāyaṁ nikkhipati aññañca kāyaṁ upādiyati tamahaṁ ‘saupavajjo’ti vadāmi.
- **EN**: When someone lays down this body and takes up another body, I call them ‘blameworthy’.

## mn144:13.10
- **PLI**: Taṁ channassa bhikkhuno natthi.
- **EN**: But the mendicant Channa did no such thing.
- **NOTE**: The Buddha confirms that Channa was an arahant.

## mn144:13.11
- **PLI**: ‘Anupavajjo channo bhikkhu satthaṁ āharesī’ti evametaṁ, sāriputta, dhārehī”ti.
- **EN**: You should remember this: ‘The mendicant Channa committed suicide blamelessly.’”
- **NOTE**: Suicide generally is condemned in Buddhism as the taking of a human life. But from this and similar incidents (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn4.23/en/sujato'>SN 4.23</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn22.87/en/sujato'>SN 22.87</a>) it is widely accepted that suicide in the final throes of a terminal illness is allowable for arahants, as they are incapable of committing a blameworthy act. The suttas, however, can also be read to support the reverse reading: the reason it is allowable for arahants is because it is not blameworthy. The underlying logic would be that ending life is normally bad because life is valuable, but at such a point life no longer has any value. Obviously this is a complex issue that cannot be resolved by a few short scriptural passages, but a Buddhist would be motivated by compassion and understanding for those going through such terrible suffering.
