## mn109:13.1  `cck14.102, dr13.98, ms11M_209, msdiv89, nya13, pts-vp-pli3.19, sya14.106, vri14.67`
- **PLI**: “Kathaṁ pana, bhante, jānato kathaṁ passato imasmiñca saviññāṇake kāye bahiddhā ca sabbanimittesu ahaṅkāramamaṅkāramānānusayā na hontī”ti?
- **EN**: “Sir, how does one know and see so that there’s no I-making, mine-making, or underlying tendency to conceit for this conscious body and externally for all signs?”
- **NOTE**: From meditative contemplation, the question now turns to realization. The Buddha’s response makes it clear he is now speaking of the penetrative understanding of the aggregates that arises at stream-entry at least. The “underlying tendency to conceit”, however, is only fully overcome by the arahant. | “I-making” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>ahaṅkāra</i>) emphasizes the active role of the mind in creating a sense of self through its thoughts, habits, and desires. This is in contrast with the English notion of “ego”, which presents self as a given construct. | “Mine-making” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>mamaṅkāra</i>) is the propensity of the mind to appropriate things as possessions. | “All signs” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sabbanimitta</i>) are the “features” (also <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nimitta</i>) that characterize sensory stimulation (eg. <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn112/en/sujato#15.1'>MN 112:15.1</a>).

## mn109:14.2
- **PLI**: “iti kira, bho, rūpaṁ anattā, vedanā anattā, saññā anattā, saṅkhārā anattā, viññāṇaṁ anattā;
- **EN**: “So it seems, worthy sir, that form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness are not-self.
- **NOTE**: In his mind, the monk addresses the Buddha with “worthy sir” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>bho</i>), which is a signature of the brahmins (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn98/en/sujato#11.3'>MN 98:11.3</a>). Small wonder he still assumes a self.

## mn109:14.3
- **PLI**: anattakatāni kammāni kamattānaṁ phusissantī”ti?
- **EN**: Then how will the deeds done by not-self affect the self?”
- **NOTE**: This monk, missing the point, thinks not-self means he can escape moral responsibility. A theory of self assumes a continuous entity, so that deeds done at one time will affect the same entity at another time. The Buddhist concept, on the other hand, is of a flow, so that, for example, pollution dumped in a stream will affect the river and the sea below, even though they are not materially the “same”.

## mn109:14.4
- **PLI**: Atha kho bhagavā tassa bhikkhuno cetasā cetoparivitakkamaññāya bhikkhū āmantesi:
- **EN**: But the Buddha, knowing that monk’s train of thought, addressed the monks:

- **EN**: “It’s possible that some futile person here—unknowing and ignorant, his mind dominated by craving—thinks he can overstep the Teacher’s instructions. He thinks:

- **EN**: ‘So it seems, worthy sir, that form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness are not-self.

## mn109:14.8
- **PLI**: Paṭivinītā kho me tumhe, bhikkhave, tatra tatra dhammesu.
- **EN**: Now, monks, you have been educated by me in questioning with regard to all these things in all such cases.
- **NOTE**: Read <i lang='pi' translate='no'>paṭipucchāvinītā</i> with <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn22.82/en/sujato#13.4'>SN 22.82:13.4</a>. It is the name for the method of questioning the Buddha is about to use. The Buddha does not just state doctrine for his students, he has “educated” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>vinīta</i>) them in the methods for learning. Active questioning breaks up long discourses and encourages reflection and articulation.
