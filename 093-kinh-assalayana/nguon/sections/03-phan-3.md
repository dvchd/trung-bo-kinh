## mn93:11.5
- **PLI**: idha rājā khattiyo muddhāvasitto nānājaccānaṁ purisānaṁ purisasataṁ sannipāteyya:
- **EN**: Suppose an anointed aristocratic king were to gather a hundred people of various births and say to them:
- **NOTE**: Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 11.5 relates how Purūravas, longing to be reunited with his queen, the nymph Urvaśī, was required by the Gandharvas to make a special fire. It must be generated by wood from a tree that grew from the ashes of celestial fire provided by themselves, the upper drill-stick of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>aśvattha</i> (i.e. Bodhi tree) and the lower stick of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>śamī</i>. In that legend, the essence of the fire is determined by its divine origin, against which the Buddha poses a naturalistic explanation.

## mn93:11.6
- **PLI**: ‘āyantu bhonto ye tattha khattiyakulā brāhmaṇakulā rājaññakulā uppannā, sākassa vā sālassa vā salaḷassa vā candanassa vā padumakassa vā uttarāraṇiṁ ādāya, aggiṁ abhinibbattentu, tejo pātukarontu.
- **EN**: ‘Please worthy sirs, let anyone here who was born in a family of aristocrats, brahmins, or chieftains take a drill-stick made of teak, sal, <i lang='pi' translate='no'>salaḷa</i>, sandalwood, or cherry wood, light a fire and produce heat.
- **NOTE**: This seems to be the only mention of the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>padumaka</i> tree in Pali. It may be the Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>padmaka</i>, for which one possible identification is <em>Prunus cerasoides</em>, the wild Himalayan cherry. | Here we find both <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sāka</i> and <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sāla</i>, so I translate <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sāka</i> as “teak”, whereas at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn3/en/sujato#1.15.10'>DN 3:1.15.10</a> I have sakhua, an alternate name for the sal tree. | For <i lang='pi' translate='no'>salaḷa</i> see note on <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn52.8/en/sujato#1.1'>SN 52.8:1.1</a>.

## mn93:11.7
- **PLI**: Āyantu pana bhonto ye tattha caṇḍālakulā nesādakulā venakulā rathakārakulā pukkusakulā uppannā, sāpānadoṇiyā vā sūkaradoṇiyā vā rajakadoṇiyā vā eraṇḍakaṭṭhassa vā uttarāraṇiṁ ādāya, aggiṁ abhinibbattentu, tejo pātukarontū’ti.
- **EN**: And let anyone here who was born in a family of corpse-workers, hunters, bamboo-workers, chariot-makers, or scavengers take a drill-stick made from a mutt’s drinking trough, a pig’s trough, a dustbin, or castor-oil wood, light a fire and produce heat.’
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Caṇḍālas</i> (“corpse-workers”) are frequently depicted as the lowest of untouchables as seen by Brahmins. However, the usual rendering of “outcast” is unsatisfactory, as all the groups here are defined positively by their occupation, and moreover some, such as the scavengers, would also have been untouchable. The Pali commentaries equate the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>caṇḍāla</i> with the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>chavaka</i> (or <i lang='pi' translate='no'>chapaka</i>) of <a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-sk69/en/sujato#1.12.1'>Bu Sk 69:1.12.1</a>, a “corpse disposer” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>chavachaḍḍakacaṇḍālā</i>; see also <i lang='pi' translate='no'>chavakacaṇḍālo</i> at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mil5.4.7/en/sujato#2.4'>Mil 5.4.7:2.4</a>). They were known for their “bone-washing” ceremony (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato#1.13.2'>DN 1:1.13.2</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an10.107/en/sujato#1.1'>AN 10.107:1.1</a>). This agrees with Rāmāyana 1.58.10, which describes a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>caṇḍāla</i> as dark-complexioned, dirty, with disheveled hair, his body smeared with graveyard ash. Mahāvaṁsa 10.91–94 tells how King Paṇḍukābhaya employed <i lang='pi' translate='no'>caṇḍālas</i> for cleaning streets and toilets, bearing the dead, and watching over cemeteries, with their village adjoining the cemetery.

## mn93:12.5
- **PLI**: idha khattiyakumāro brāhmaṇakaññāya saddhiṁ saṁvāsaṁ kappeyya, tesaṁ saṁvāsamanvāya putto jāyetha;
