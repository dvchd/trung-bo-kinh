## mn31:1.2
- **PLI**: ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā nātike viharati giñjakāvasathe.
- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying at Ñātika in the brick house.
- **NOTE**: Ñātika (also spelled <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nātika</i>, or <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nādika</i>; Sanskrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>jñātṛka</i>; Prākrit <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nāyika</i>) was the clan to which the Jain leader Mahāvīra (called Nāṭaputta, i.e. Ñātiputta) belonged. This is the chief town of the clan. It was part of the Vajjian Federation (See <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato#2.5.1'>DN 16:2.5.1</a>). | Over a millennium previously, the Indus Valley Civilization had built cities of fired brick with standardized size and construction methods, but in the Buddha’s day most buildings were wood. This is the only brick building mentioned in the suttas, although brick construction is also discussed in the Vinaya.

## mn31:2.1  `nya2`
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena āyasmā ca anuruddho āyasmā ca nandiyo āyasmā ca kimilo gosiṅgasālavanadāye viharanti.
- **EN**: Now at that time the venerables Anuruddha, Nandiya, and Kimbila were staying in the sal forest park at Gosiṅga.
- **NOTE**: Anuruddha was the brother of Ānanda and Mahānāma (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd17/en/sujato#1.1.3'>Kd 17:1.1.3</a>) which, according to the commentary, makes the Buddha his cousin. He was an exponent of mindfulness meditation (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn52.1/en/sujato'>SN 52.1</a>, etc.) and master of clairvoyance (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an1.192/en/sujato#1.1'>AN 1.192:1.1</a>). | Kimbila was one of the seven leading Sakyans, including Anuruddha and Ānanda, who went forth together (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd17/en/sujato#1.4.1'>Kd 17:1.4.1</a>). | Several Nandiyas are known in the suttas, one of whom was a Sakyan (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn55.40/en/sujato'>SN 55.40</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an11.13/en/sujato'>AN 11.13</a>; the root <i lang='pi' translate='no'>nand</i> (“joy”) was a popular choice for Sakyan names: Ānanda, Nanda, Upananda). ## mn31:3.5
- **PLI**: Santettha tayo kulaputtā attakāmarūpā viharanti.
- **EN**: There are three gentlemen staying here whose nature is to desire self-knowledge.
- **NOTE**: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Attakāmarūpa</i> is glossed by the commentary as “those whose nature is to desire their own welfare”. In the Vinaya (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>attakāma</i>) means to serve “one’s own desires” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-ss4/en/sujato'>Bu Ss 4</a>). But here it draws on Yājnavalkya’s description of one whose form is such that, having attained their only desire, the Self, they have become without desire (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>ātmakāmam āptakāmam akāmaṁ rūpaṁ</i>, Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.7.1.21, Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.21. Cf. <i lang='pi' translate='no'>niṣkāma āptakāma ātmakāmo</i>, Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 14.7.2.8, Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.4.6. In both cases the Bṛhadāraṇyaka reverses the sequence of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>āpta</i> … <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ātma</i>.) This describes a sage who is fully immersed in the realization of Brahman in death or dreamless sleep. Such realization is a form of “knowledge” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>veda</i>). Here the suffix <i lang='pi' translate='no'>-rūpa</i> does not mean “seemingly”, nor is it to be discarded as a mere idiom, but has the sense given in the commentary, a person’s true form or true nature (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sabhāva</i>). This is, of course, not a Buddhist concept. But it is spoken by the keeper of a park (not a monastery) who fails to even recognize the Buddha. There is no reason he should be Buddhist. To this day, Buddhist monastics are received with kindness and unhesitatingly supported by Hindus, who readily interpret and describe the monastics’ practice in their own terms.

## mn31:6.1  `nya6`
- **PLI**: “Kacci pana vo, anuruddhā, samaggā sammodamānā avivadamānā khīrodakībhūtā aññamaññaṁ piyacakkhūhi sampassantā viharathā”ti?
- **EN**: “I hope you’re living in harmony, appreciating each other, without quarreling, blending like milk and water, and regarding each other with kindly eyes?”
- **NOTE**: The emphasis on harmonious living fits the accounts in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd10/en/sujato#4.2.1'>Kd 10:4.2.1</a> and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn128/en/sujato'>MN 128</a>, where the Buddha had just left the quarreling community of Kosambī. This suggests that the introduction belongs there, when the three friends were practicing together in the Eastern Bamboo Park near Kosambī. The current sutta is set around 400 km to the east near Vesālī, and it is hardly likely that the Buddha went straight there after leaving Kosambī. In addition, in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn128/en/sujato'>MN 128</a> the three friends are still developing their meditation, whereas in the current sutta they have already achieved arahantship. Also, the location there is said to be the site of a hall for Vedic rituals, which suits the evidently non-Buddhist park keeper. It seems that the popular introduction of <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn128/en/sujato'>MN 128</a> was reused to frame a later conversation with the three friends, despite the implausibility of the same events happening in the same way twice.
