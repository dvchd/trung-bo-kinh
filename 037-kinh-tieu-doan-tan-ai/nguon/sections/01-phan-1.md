## mn37:3.2
- **PLI**: ‘sabbe dhammā nālaṁ abhinivesāyā’ti.
- **EN**: ‘Nothing is worth insisting on.’
- **NOTE**: The Buddha is calling back to the previous conversation, where immediately before asking about the ultimate goal, Sakka had asked why all ascetics did not share a common doctrine (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn21/en/sujato#2.6.6'>DN 21:2.6.6</a>). ## mn37:3.5
- **PLI**: So sabbaṁ dhammaṁ abhijānāti; sabbaṁ dhammaṁ abhiññāya sabbaṁ dhammaṁ parijānāti; sabbaṁ dhammaṁ pariññāya yaṁ kiñci vedanaṁ vedeti—
- **EN**: they directly know all things. Directly knowing all things, they completely understand all things. Completely understanding all things, when they experience any kind of feeling—pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral—
- **NOTE**: One who has learned the theory of non-attachment is still liable to become attached, so they apply their knowledge to experiential insight into “all things”. Normally “complete understanding” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>pariññā</i>) indicates arahantship, but here it reinforces “direct knowledge” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>abhiññā</i>) as the wisdom of stream-entry. However, the force of attachment is so deeply ingrained that even a stream-enterer, such as Sakka, with both theoretical and experiential wisdom is still not free. So they must then continue to meditate on the feelings in order to let go fully.

It is used by the Buddha in “dismissing” a badly behaved Saṅgha (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn67/en/sujato#5.1'>MN 67:5.1</a>). ## mn37:8.1  `nya8`
- **PLI**: “yathā kathaṁ pana kho, kosiya, bhagavā saṅkhittena taṇhāsaṅkhayavimuttiṁ abhāsi?
- **EN**: “Kosiya, how did the Buddha briefly explain freedom through the ending of craving?
- **NOTE**: Moggallāna refers to Sakka as “Kosiya” rather than the Buddha’s “lord of gods”. Old gods like Sakka tend to accrue many names. The word <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kosiya</i> is explained by the commentaries as “owl”, which, if correct, would have been the totem for a clan of that name. It is, however, a patronymic: Rig Veda 1.10.11 has <i lang='pi' translate='no'>indra kauśika</i> which means “Indra, son of Kuśika (or Kuśa)”. <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Kuśa</i> grass is critical to the performance of Vedic rites, and the label probably initially implied “Brahmanized”, i.e. a king whose reign was authorized according to Vedic ritual. Kosiya is said to be a low class family name (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-pc2/en/sujato#2.1.18'>Bu Pc 2:2.1.18</a>).

## mn37:9.3
- **PLI**: disvā ottappamānā hirīyamānā sakaṁ sakaṁ ovarakaṁ pavisiṁsu.
- **EN**: being discreet and prudent, each went to her own bedroom.
- **NOTE**: The maids are described as possessing the cardinal virtues of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>hiri</i> (conscience, discretion) and <i lang='pi' translate='no'>otappa</i> (prudence), the “guardians of the world” and the foundations of moral integrity. Former translators have rendered this as if the maids were “embarrassed and ashamed”, but this is unwarranted. The maids were born in this realm as the result of their good kamma in past lives and are simply residing there in their own home. Unlike Sakka, who neglected his lesson and then tried to distract attention when reminded of it, they have nothing to be ashamed of.
