- **PLI**: Evaṁ me sutaṁ—
- **EN**: So I have heard.
- **NOTE**: This is one of the most famous discourses in studies of early Buddhism, largely due to the ground-breaking analysis by Venerable Kaṭukurunde Ñāṇananda in his 1971 monograph <em>Concept and Reality in Early Buddhist Thought</em>, which established the meaning of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>papañca</i> as “conceptual proliferation”. | It is the first of the “thought trilogy”, a series of discourses that deal with the activity of thinking in meditation (also <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn19/en/sujato'>MN 19</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn20/en/sujato'>MN 20</a>).

## mn18:1.2
- **PLI**: ekaṁ samayaṁ bhagavā sakkesu viharati kapilavatthusmiṁ nigrodhārāme.
- **EN**: At one time the Buddha was staying in the land of the Sakyans, near Kapilavatthu in the Banyan Tree Monastery.
- **NOTE**: The Banyan Tree Monastery (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>nigrodhārāma</i>) was the normal residence for the Buddha and his disciples in the Sakyan republic. It was named, according to northern traditions, after the banyan trees that grew there, while the Pali commentaries say it was named after a Sakyan called Nigrodha who donated it. The two stories are not incompatible, as the owner could have been known by his most famous attribute, his banyan grove. It has been identified by stupas located next to the village Kudan, just north of the India-Nepal border.

- **EN**: Then the Buddha robed up in the morning and, taking his bowl and robe, entered Kapilavatthu for alms.

## mn18:2.2
- **PLI**: Kapilavatthusmiṁ piṇḍāya caritvā pacchābhattaṁ piṇḍapātapaṭikkanto yena mahāvanaṁ tenupasaṅkami divāvihārāya.
- **EN**: He wandered for alms in Kapilavatthu. After the meal, on his return from almsround, he went to the Great Wood for the day’s meditation,
- **NOTE**: The Great Wood (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>mahāvana</i>) was a favorite meditation place of the Buddha. The commentaries say it was a stretch of wilderness that reached as far as the Himalayas on one side (200 km) and the ocean on the other (1000 km). Later tradition says that a town should have three woods: a “great wood” for wilderness (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>mahāvana</i>); a “prosperity wood” for resources (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>sirivana</i>); and an “ascetic wood” for spiritual practice (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>tapovana</i>).

## mn18:3.1  `nya3`
- **PLI**: Daṇḍapāṇipi kho sakko jaṅghāvihāraṁ anucaṅkamamāno anuvicaramāno yena mahāvanaṁ tenupasaṅkami.
- **EN**: Daṇḍapāṇi the Sakyan, while going for a walk,
- **NOTE**: Daṇḍapāṇi was said to be the brother of the Buddha’s birth mother Māyā and foster mother Mahāpajāpatī (Mahāvaṁsa 2.19), or else the father of the Buddha’s former wife Yasodharā (or Gopā, Lalitavistara 12.15). Both could be true, making Siddhattha’s wife his cousin. Reading between the lines, it seems Daṇḍapāṇi nursed a grudge against the Buddha. This would be understandable if Siddhattha’s birth resulted in the death of one of Daṇḍapāṇi’s sisters, while the other sister was left distraught when he went forth; and even more so if he abandoned Daṇḍapāṇi’s daughter with their newborn son.

## mn18:3.4
- **PLI**: “kiṁvādī samaṇo kimakkhāyī”ti?
- **EN**: “What is the ascetic’s doctrine? What does he assert?”
- **NOTE**: There is a minor Vinaya training against teaching anyone with “a staff in their hand” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>daṇḍapāṇi</i>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-bu-vb-sk58/en/sujato#1.3.1'>Bu Sk 58:1.3.1</a>). This was evidently laid down because a staff could be used as a weapon, and hence was associated with royal authority or with policing and the exercise of violence. In a vision interpreted at Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 11.6.1.13, a man with staff in hand is identified with wrath (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>krodha</i>).

## mn18:4.1  `nya4`
- **PLI**: “Yathāvādī kho, āvuso, sadevake loke samārake sabrahmake sassamaṇabrāhmaṇiyā pajāya sadevamanussāya na kenaci loke viggayha tiṭṭhati, yathā ca pana kāmehi visaṁyuttaṁ viharantaṁ taṁ brāhmaṇaṁ akathaṅkathiṁ chinnakukkuccaṁ bhavābhave vītataṇhaṁ saññā nānusenti—
- **EN**: “Respectable sir, my doctrine is such that one does not argue with anyone in this world with its gods, Māras, and divinities, this population with its ascetics and brahmins, its gods and humans. And it is such that perceptions do not underlie the brahmin who lives detached from sensual pleasures, without indecision, stripped of worry, and rid of craving for rebirth in this or that state.
- **NOTE**: In the face of Daṇḍapāṇi’s evidently hostile attitude, the Buddha addresses his uncle with the respectful <i lang='pi' translate='no'>āvuso</i> (“sir”), and emphasizes non-conflict in line with his claim that, “I do not argue with the world; it is the world that argues with me” (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn22.94/en/sujato'>SN 22.94</a>). | The expression “perceptions do not underlie” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>saññā nānusenti</i>) is unique to this context and must pertain to the highly-charged relation between the Buddha and Daṇḍapāṇi. “Perception” is that mode of knowing that interprets the present in terms of the past, and hence it might sometimes be translated as “recognition”. The Buddha, by asserting he is no longer bound by past perceptions, is hinting that this is how Daṇḍapāṇi can get over his grudge.

## mn18:8.1  `nya8`
- **PLI**: “Yatonidānaṁ, bhikkhu, purisaṁ papañcasaññāsaṅkhā samudācaranti.
- **EN**: “Mendicant, judgments driven by proliferating perceptions beset a person.
- **NOTE**: “Judgment” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>saṅkhā</i>) is the way we “appraise” or “assess” ourselves, especially in relation to others (cf. <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn1/en/sujato#3.3'>MN 1:3.3</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato#1.3.2'>DN 1:1.3.2</a>). | “Proliferation” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>papañca</i>) is the compulsion of the mind to spread out in endless inner commentary that hides reality. | “Beset” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>samudācaranti</i>) conveys the sense that the person is overwhelmed and swamped, no longer the agent of their existence. | A “person” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>purisa</i>) is the conventional sense of self that arises from desire and identification.
