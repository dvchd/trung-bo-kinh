## mn95:11.2
- **PLI**: Tena kho pana samayena kāpaṭiko nāma māṇavo daharo vuttasiro soḷasavassuddesiko jātiyā, tiṇṇaṁ vedānaṁ pāragū sanighaṇḍukeṭubhānaṁ sākkharappabhedānaṁ itihāsapañcamānaṁ, padako, veyyākaraṇo, lokāyatamahāpurisalakkhaṇesu anavayo tassaṁ parisāyaṁ nisinno hoti.
- **EN**: And the student Kāpaṭika was sitting in that assembly. He was young, tonsured, and sixteen years old. He had mastered the three Vedas, together with their vocabularies and ritual performance, their phonology and word classification, and the testaments as fifth. He knew them word-by-word, and their grammar. He was well versed in cosmology and the marks of a great man.
- **NOTE**: This student’s education is like that of Assalāyana (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn93/en/sujato#3.1'>MN 93:3.1</a>), but his character could not be more different. His name has a variety of spellings in Pali and Sanskrit, but the correct form is <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kāpaṭika</i> from <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kapaṭa</i>, “fraud”. Kauṭilya explains that fraudulent students (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>chātraḥ kāpaṭikaḥ</i>) were spies installed by kings to test the loyalty of subjects (Arthaśāstra 1.10.11, 1.11.1–2), for which they were paid handsomely (5.3.22). Note that the Buddha calls him Bhāradvāja; this follows the convention in Pali that brahmins of that name are distinguished by epithets, often unflattering: Bhāradvāja the Farmer (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp1.4/en/sujato#1.3'>Snp 1.4:1.3</a>), Bhāradvāja the Fire-Worshiper (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp1.7/en/sujato#1.4'>Snp 1.7:1.4</a>), Bhāradvāja the Alms-Gatherer (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn35.127/en/sujato#1.1'>SN 35.127:1.1</a>), Bhāradvāja the Builder (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn7.17/en/sujato#1.2'>SN 7.17:1.2</a>), Bhāradvāja the Rude (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn7.2/en/sujato#1.2'>SN 7.2:1.2</a>), Bhāradvāja the Fiend (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn7.3/en/sujato#1.2'>SN 7.3:1.2</a>), Bhāradvāja the Bitter (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn7.4/en/sujato#1.2'>SN 7.4:1.2</a>), and so on. Thus Kāpaṭika would have been an epithet recognizing that he was the king’s spy, a practice to which Pasenadi openly admits (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn3.11/en/sujato#7.1'>SN 3.11:7.1</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ud6.2/en/sujato#8.1'>Ud 6.2:8.1</a>). This was the downside of building a religious institution on the king’s largess. Whether he was recognized as a spy at the time, or the epithet was applied later, is uncertain, but at some point it seems the meaning was forgotten and the epithet was taken to be his proper name.
