## mn116:6.5  `ms11M_379`

- **PLI**: Atha kosallo buddho atho subāhu;

## mn116:6.7
- **PLI**: Upanemiso nemiso santacitto,
- **EN**: Upanemi, Nemi, and Santacitta,
- **NOTE**: Nemi (“rim”) was the name of the 22nd Jain <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i>, where it is an abbreviation of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ariṣṭanemi</i> (“indestructible rim”). | Take <i lang='pi' translate='no'>so</i> here as connective pronoun.

## mn116:6.8
- **PLI**: Sacco tatho virajo paṇḍito ca.
- **EN**: right and true, stainless and astute.

## mn116:6.9  `bj12.210, csp1ed11.115, csp2ed11.115, ms11M_380, sya14.178`

## mn116:6.10
- **PLI**: Aṅgo ca paṅgo ca guttijito ca;
- **EN**: Aṅga and Paṅga, and Guttijita too;
- **NOTE**: Aṅga (“limb”) is a kingdom, and is sometimes found as a personal name. | Paṅga may be a variant spelling of Vaṅga, i.e. Bengal. | Guttijita means “guard and conqueror (of the senses)”.

## mn116:6.11
- **PLI**: Passi jahi upadhidukkhamūlaṁ,
- **EN**: Passin gave up attachment, suffering’s root,
- **NOTE**: Passi most obviously means “seer”. But perhaps it could be from the root <i lang='pi' translate='no'>passa</i> (“side”), making this the 23rd Jain <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i> Pārśva, who immediately preceded Mahāvīra.

## mn116:6.12
- **PLI**: Aparājito mārabalaṁ ajesi.
- **EN**: while Aparājita defeated Māra’s power.
- **NOTE**: Aparājita (“unconquered by another”) is a common name. It was the name of Śāntinātha, the 16th Jain <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i>, in a past life.

## mn116:6.13  `dr13.162, ms11M_381`
- **PLI**: Satthā pavattā sarabhaṅgo lomahaṁso,
- **EN**: Satthar, Pavattar, Sarabhaṅga, Lomahaṁsa,
- **NOTE**: Satthar (“teacher”) is used in both Buddhism (usually for the Buddha) and Jainism. Pavattar (“roller-forth”, “proclaimer”) has a similar sense. | Sarabhaṅga (“arrow-breaker”) was a forest hermit in Daṇḍaka. See note at <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato#13.27'>MN 56:13.27</a>. | Lomahaṁsa means “hair raising”.

## mn116:6.14
- **PLI**: Uccaṅgamāyo asito anāsavo;
- **EN**: Uccaṅgamāya, Asita, Anāsava,
- **NOTE**: Asita (“dark one”) was the name of the sage who attended Siddhattha after his birth (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp3.11/en/sujato'>Snp 3.11</a>), while an Asita Devala also features in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn93/en/sujato'>MN 93</a>. The Rig Veda attributes several hymns to Asita or Devala (sometimes said to be his son) of the Kāśyapa clan. Asita Devala also appears in the Jain Isibhāsiyāiṁ chapter 3. | Anāsava means “undefiled”. Freedom from “defilement” is a key concept in Jainism.

## mn116:6.15
- **PLI**: Manomayo mānacchido ca bandhumā,
- **EN**: Manomaya, and Bandhumā the cutter of conceit,

## mn116:6.16
- **PLI**: Tadādhimutto vimalo ca ketumā.
- **EN**: then Adhimutta, and Ketumā the immaculate.
- **NOTE**: The commentary says <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tadādhimutto</i> is the only name in this line, but I take <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tad(ā)</i> as a connective pronoun, leaving <i lang='pi' translate='no'>adhimutta</i> (“faithful”) as the name. Moreover, since the syntax echoes the previous line, I construe it the same way, making <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ketumā</i> a proper name, meaning one who is “adorned with banners” (like the sun). There are several people and places of this name in Sanskrit, among them at least one sage; it corresponds to Ketu in Mahāvastu 21. See also <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ketumatī</i>, said to be an old name of Varanasi (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn26/en/sujato#23.8'>DN 26:23.8</a>). On the other hand, I follow the commentary in taking <i lang='pi' translate='no'>vimalo</i> as an attribute (“immaculate”), but it could be a proper name, in which case it would be shared with Vimala, the 13th Jain <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i>.

## mn116:6.17  `ms11M_382, ndp11.134`
- **PLI**: Ketumbharāgo ca mātaṅgo ariyo,
- **EN**: Ketumbharāga, Mātaṅga, and Ariya,
- **NOTE**: Ketumbharāga is “cloud-colored”. | Mātaṅga (“one who goes where they wish”, i.e. “elephant”) is the name of an outcast who became a sage (Skandapurāṇa 5.2.60). In other Buddhist versions of this story, he ascends to the Brahmā realm (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp1.7/en/sujato#26.1'>Snp 1.7:26.1</a>), in which case he is identified with the Bodhisatta (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja497/en/sujato'>Ja 497</a>). This contradicts the present sutta, for a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>paccekabuddha</i> is not reborn anywhere. Either there were several sages of this name, or else the memory of a past sage was rather vaguely specified. In the Jain Isibhāsiyāiṁ chapter 26, he criticizes so-called Brahmins who practice violence. See too note on <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato#13.27'>MN 56:13.27</a>. | A sage named Ariya is found in Isibhāsiyāiṁ 19, where he teaches a doctrine of “civility”.

## mn116:6.18
- **PLI**: Athaccuto accutagāmabyāmako;
- **EN**: then Accuta, Accutagāma, and Byāmaka,
- **NOTE**: Accuta (“unfallen”) is a name of Agni (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 1.6.1.6) and later Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa. | If Accuta is Viṣṇu/Kṛṣṇa, perhaps Accutagāma is a garbled form of his elder brother Balarāma’s name Acyutāgraja (“firstborn of the unfallen”). | Byāmaka means “fathom-long”.

## mn116:6.19
- **PLI**: Sumaṅgalo dabbilo supatiṭṭhito,
- **EN**: Sumaṅgala, Dabbila, Supatiṭṭhita,
- **NOTE**: Sumaṅgala (“good fortune”) is a common name. A Jain text tells of a king of that name who became an ascetic in later life (Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 10.6.2). <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja420/en/sujato'>Ja 420</a> tells of how a park-keeper named Sumaṅgala accidentally killed a <i lang='pi' translate='no'>paccekabuddha</i>. | Dabbila probably refers to one who uses the sacrificial “ladle” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>darvi</i>, see <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn93/en/sujato#18.71'>MN 93:18.71</a> and note). | Supatiṭṭhita (“well-established”) might be an attribute; commentary is silent, but Mahāvastu 21 takes it as a name.

## mn116:6.20
- **PLI**: Asayho khemābhirato ca sorato.
- **EN**: Asayha, Khemābhirata, and Sorata.

## mn116:6.21  `ms11M_383, vri14.117`
- **PLI**: Durannayo saṅgho athopi ujjayo,
- **EN**: Durannaya, Saṅgha, and also Ujjaya,
- **NOTE**: Durannaya means “hard to follow”. | Saṅgha (“community”) is a fairly common name in Pali. | Ujjaya (“victory”) in Pali is the name of a brahmin (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/an4.39/en/sujato'>AN 4.39</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/an8.55/en/sujato'>AN 8.55</a>) and a mendicant (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/thag1.47/en/sujato'>Thag 1.47</a>). The city of Ujjeni in Avantī is sometimes spelled <i lang='pi' translate='no'>ujjaya</i>, so this is perhaps someone from there.

## mn116:6.22

- **EN**: another sage, Sayha of peerless effort.
- **NOTE**: Sayha is the name of a mountain district in which the Godāvarī arises (Trimbakeshwar Range). It is south of Ujjeni, so perhaps both of these are places.

## mn116:6.23
- **PLI**: Ānando nando upanando dvādasa,
- **EN**: There are twelve Ānandas, Nandas, and Upanandas,
- **NOTE**: The commentary says there were four of each, making twelve in total. All are common names meaning “joy”.

## mn116:6.24
- **PLI**: Bhāradvājo antimadehadhārī.
- **EN**: and Bhāradvāja, bearing his final body.
- **NOTE**: Bhāradvāja, one of the seven great Vedic sages, founded a storied lineage (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn32/en/sujato#10.3'>DN 32:10.3</a>, <a href='https://suttacentral.net/mn95/en/sujato#11.2'>MN 95:11.2</a>).

## mn116:6.25  `cck14.169, ms11M_384, sya14.179`
- **PLI**: Bodhi mahānāmo athopi uttaro,
- **EN**: Bodhi, Mahānāma, and also Uttara,

## mn116:6.26
- **PLI**: Kesī sikhī sundaro dvārabhājo;
- **EN**: Kesī, Sikhī, Sundara, and Bhāradvāja,
- **NOTE**: In Jainism, Keśi is a noted ascetic disciple of Pārśva (Uttarādhyayanasūtra 23.2). | Sikhī was a former Buddha (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn6.14/en/sujato'>SN 6.14</a>), whose name is solar in connotation (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn14/en/sujato#1.12.4'>DN 14:1.12.4</a>): the streaming radiance of the sun. The sun is also said to be <i lang='pi' translate='no'>kesi</i> (“hairy”) for the same reason (eg. Rig Veda 10.136.1), so these two names go together. | Sundara (“beautiful”) is a common name. | The presence of a second Bhāradvāja creates tension, since duplicates are mostly avoided. The Mahāsaṅgīti resolves it by reading <i lang='pi' translate='no'>dvārabhāja</i> here, which is otherwise unattested. In her translation, Horner took <i lang='pi' translate='no'>athopi uttaro</i> in the previous line as “also another” Bhāradvāja, with the other items as attributes. However, I leave the tension unresolved, as there were many Bhāradvājas.

## mn116:6.27
- **PLI**: Tissūpatissā bhavabandhanacchidā,
- **EN**: Tissa and Upatissa, <j>who’ve both cut the bonds to rebirth,
- **NOTE**: Tissa is a common name, meaning “born under the star Sirius”. Notably, Tissa follows Bhāradvāja; both were names of the chief disciples of Kassapa Buddha, and were associated with Mount Vepulla of Rājagaha in a former age (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn15.20/en/sujato#4.6'>SN 15.20:4.6</a>).

## mn116:6.28
- **PLI**: Upasikhi taṇhacchido ca sikhari.
- **EN**: Upasīdarī and Sīdarī, who’ve both cut off craving.
- **NOTE**: Sīdarī perhaps means “dweller by the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>Sīdā</i>”, which in Pali tradition is a river in the Himalayas beside which ascetics flourish (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja541/en/sujato#16.1'>Ja 541:16.1</a>). It is probably a variant spelling of <i lang='pi' translate='no'>sītā</i> (“cold”), a common name for rivers. The tenth Jain <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i> has the similar name Śītala.

- **NOTE**: Uposatha (“sabbath”) is the name of King Sudassana’s royal elephant (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/dn17/en/sujato#2.5.5'>DN 17:2.5.5</a>). | This is the second Sundara; one or both might be an attribute. | Saccanāma (“one whose name is true”) recalls the famous sage Satyakāma Jābāla of Chāndogya Upaniṣad 4.4.

## mn116:6.33  `dr13.163, ms11M_386, pts-vp-pli3.71`
- **PLI**: Jeto jayanto padumo uppalo ca,
- **EN**: Jeta, Jayanta, Paduma, and Uppala;
- **NOTE**: All these are common names. Jeta and Jayanta both mean “victor”, while Paduma and Uppala both mean “lotus”. In Jainism, Paduma recalls the name of the sixth <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i>, Padmaprabha (“lotus-shine”), while a sage named Jayanta practiced in the mountains during a previous incarnation of the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkara</i> Śanti (Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 5.1.9).

## mn116:6.34
- **PLI**: Padumuttaro rakkhito pabbato ca;
- **EN**: Padumuttara, Rakkhita, and Pabbata,
- **NOTE**: Padumuttara (“supreme lotus”) is well known in Theravāda as the name of the thirteenth of the twenty-eight Buddhas. Jains recognize two sages of this name, as incarnations of the <i lang='pi' translate='no'>tīrthaṅkaras</i> Śītala and Vāsupūjya (Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 3.8.1, 4.2.1). | Rakkhita (“protected”) is common name. Here it is followed by another place name, Pabbata (“mountain”). A sage Rakkhita of the mountains features in the related Jātaka stories told at Mahāvastu 28a and <a href='https://suttacentral.net/ja505/en/sujato'>Ja 505</a>. Parvata is also the name of a Vedic poet, who legend says was a nephew of Nārada.

## mn116:6.35

- **NOTE**: Mānatthaddha means “stiff with pride”; a brahmin of that name appears in <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn7.15/en/sujato'>SN 7.15</a>. Presumably it was an old nickname that stuck.

## mn116:6.36
