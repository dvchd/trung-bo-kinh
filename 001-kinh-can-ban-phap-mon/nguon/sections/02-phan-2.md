- **PLI**: Ākāsānañcāyatanaṁ ākāsānañcāyatanato sañjānāti;
- **EN**: They perceive the dimension of infinite space as the dimension of infinite space.
- **NOTE**: Here begins the series of realms associated with the practice of formless meditations. These were practiced by the most advanced non-dualist Brahmanical teachers before the Buddha, who identified such experiences with the highest Self that is the cosmic divinity. The Buddha adopted the practices as part of the development of meditation, divesting them of metaphysical significance.

## mn1:19.1  `csp1ed9.4, csp2ed9.4, ms9M_19, msdiv5, nya19`
- **PLI**: Diṭṭhaṁ diṭṭhato sañjānāti;
- **EN**: They perceive the seen as the seen.
- **NOTE**: Now the discourse introduces the “epistemic tetrad”—the seen, heard, thought, and known—which describe the means of knowing spiritual truths (see also note on <a href='https://suttacentral.net/sn24.1/en/sujato#4.11'>SN 24.1:4.11</a>). This framework was adopted from Yājñavalkya, for whom it is the Self that must be seen, heard, thought, and known (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.7, 2.4.5, 4.5.6; cf. Chāndogya Upaniṣad 3.13.7). Yet the Absolute Self is “the unseen seer, the unheard hearer, the unthought thinker, the unknown knower” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.4.2, 3.7.23, 3.8.11, 4.3.31), the divinity that is ancient and primordial (4.4.18). It is through inference or understanding that one can learn to see, hear, think, and know that which is unknown (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.1.3), and which comprises one’s spiritual understanding (Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.4.5).

## mn1:19.2
- **PLI**: diṭṭhaṁ diṭṭhato saññatvā diṭṭhaṁ maññati, diṭṭhasmiṁ maññati, diṭṭhato maññati, diṭṭhaṁ meti maññati, diṭṭhaṁ abhinandati.
- **EN**: Having perceived the seen as the seen, they conceive it to be the seen …
- **NOTE**: While the “sight of ascetics” is regarded as a blessing (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp2.4/en/sujato#10.2'>Snp 2.4:10.2</a>), taking the “seeing” of a holy person as a standard of truth is a mistake (<a href='https://suttacentral.net/snp4.4/en/sujato'>Snp 4.4</a>). For Vedists, seeing can also refer to the Self which is beheld in a dream (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.9), where things are revealed in a person’s own light (4.3.14).

## mn1:23.1  `bj10.10, ms9M_23, msdiv6, nya23, vri12.5`
- **PLI**: Ekattaṁ ekattato sañjānāti;
- **EN**: They perceive oneness as oneness.
- **NOTE**: Perception of “oneness” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>ekatta</i>) sees the world as manifold reflections of an underlying unity. Arising from meditative experience or philosophical reflection, it is associated with the non-dual schools of Brahmanism. Īśa Upaniṣad 7, for example, speaks of “contemplating the oneness” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>ekatvam anupaśyataḥ</i>) of all creatures with the supreme soul. Yājñavalkya said in the state of non-differentiation the Self “becomes clear as water, one, the seer without a second; this is the Brahmā realm” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 4.3.32: <i lang='pi' translate='no'>salila eko draṣṭādvaito bhavati, eṣa brahmalokaḥ</i>).

## mn1:24.1  `ms9M_24, nya24`
- **PLI**: Nānattaṁ nānattato sañjānāti;
- **EN**: They perceive diversity as diversity.
- **NOTE**: “Diversity” (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>nānatta</i>) is the opposite fallacy to “oneness”; based on the diversity of sense experience, it interprets the world as irreducibly manifold. An example would be the Jains, who believed the world was made up of countless separate entities, a view criticized in Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 3.13 (<i lang='pi' translate='no'>nānātvaṁ nindyate</i>). Both these fallacies take a particular mode of perception which is true in certain respects and make it into a metaphysical absolute.
